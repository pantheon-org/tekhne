# Planning, Decision-Making, Documentation, Reporting

Paraphrased from Jerry Madden, NASA GSFC — "Lessons Learned for Project Managers" (LLIS #1956), <https://llis.nasa.gov/lesson/1956>. Consult the source for Madden's original wording and full
elaboration.

Use during ticket refinement, plan drafting, and documentation decisions — pick the 1-3 that fit, don't dump the list.

- Wrong decisions made early can usually be fixed; "right" decisions made late often cannot.
- Bring action plans, not excuses.
- Don't retaliate against another project or team for a slight — it drags your own work down to their level.
- Guard against ego rigidity. Make sure your team feels safe telling you when you're wrong.
- Even your surest facts can turn out wrong — hold conclusions loosely until they're tested.
- Success or failure isn't a pure competence signal. Luck plays a part, but it favors the competent and hard-working.
- Documentation isn't knowledge — it's a static, quickly outdated snapshot of what was supposed to be true.
- You have to understand the language of your domain and its interfaces. Invest in that literacy deliberately.
- Build in schedule contingency. An unsolved problem doesn't disappear — it just gets inherited by whoever replaces you.
- Use abbreviations sparingly outside your own team. They exist to inform, not to signal or confuse.
- Running isn't thinking. Make time to understand the consequences of an action, not just to move fast on it.
- "Next year," with adequate funding and schedule, has a way of never actually arriving.
- On-time, in-budget, low-risk, and state-of-the-art only hold together together when the funding and schedule ground rules are set and held firm up front.
- Most overruns come from poor estimates, not mistakes. Better estimating protects reputation even if it doesn't lower cost.
- People who monitor work without helping deliver it rarely understand what's actually happening.
- Almost anyone can manage a project except the chronic procrastinator or the person who can't commit to a call.
- Good technicians and quality inspectors matter more to the outcome than paperwork and reviews do.
- A comfortable project manager is either between assignments or about to fail. Comfort isn't the normal state here.
- False starts are common. Stay ready to move decisively once the real signal actually comes.
- Real decisions are rarely made in the meeting itself — they're usually settled beforehand and staged for the room.
- A spoken agreement isn't a documented one. Write down what matters; people and their memories move on.
