# Customer/Stakeholder Relations

Paraphrased from Jerry Madden, NASA GSFC — "Lessons Learned for Project Managers" (LLIS #1956), <https://llis.nasa.gov/lesson/1956>. Consult the source for Madden's original wording and full
elaboration.

Use before drafting stakeholder comms, an incident status update for non-engineers, or vendor/partner communications — pick the 1-3 that fit, don't dump the list.

- Know your customer's actual objectives, and check in with them before any significant change.
- Programs compete for a shared pool of budget, not against each other directly. Sell your own work on its merits rather than at another team's expense.
- Identify the real decision-makers, wherever they actually sit, and build a line of communication to them.
- Other teams' or centers' resources are often available just for the asking.
- Deal fairly with everyone. The professional community is small, and you'll cross paths with the same people again.
- Meetings conducted in a shared second language need explicit confirmation of understanding, not assumed agreement.
- Challenge policy or process documents that don't make sense. They were written by someone with no more authority than you.
- A contracted scientist or specialist is still your customer, sometimes with more access to senior leadership than you have.
- People stay rational and cooperative unless something threatens their core objective. Honesty earns their cooperation; evasiveness costs it.
- Cooperative efforts need early, proactive communication. Tell partners first, and consult before anything is finalized.
- A proposal or plan can take months to write and years to approve. By the time work actually starts, drift from the original design is normal, not a red flag on its own.
- Everyone above you in the chain has their own job to do. Yours is keeping all of them reasonably satisfied at once.
- Paperwork volume tends to grow, and output per pound of effort tends to shrink, as an organization ages — worth noticing before it becomes the norm.
- Once the pioneering phase of a domain is over, the remaining work needs more discipline, while still leaving room for innovation.
- In organizational politics, look for the political motive, not the stated logic.
- Interagency or cross-team agreements often fail even without a real conflict of interest. Don't assume goodwill alone will close them.
- With outside partners, align privately ahead of time, then present a unified front in the room — keep disputes behind closed doors.
- Wanting state-of-the-art, innovative, risk-tolerant work that still lands on schedule and cost is a tall order — call that out explicitly rather than pretending it's simple.
- Starving a project or team down past a sustainable level doesn't produce a leaner version of it. Eventually it just stops working.
