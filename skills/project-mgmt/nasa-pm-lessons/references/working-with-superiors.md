# Working with Superiors

Paraphrased from Jerry Madden, NASA GSFC — "Lessons Learned for Project Managers" (LLIS #1956), <https://llis.nasa.gov/lesson/1956>. Consult the source for Madden's original wording and full
elaboration.

Use before posting a status comment to management or making an escalation call — pick the 1-3 that fit, don't dump the list.

- You and your manager are a team. They're your advocate and conduit upward, and should help keep you tied into decisions above your level too.
- Don't hand upward a decision that's actually yours to make. Assume you have the authority unless a document explicitly says otherwise.
- Managers who lean on paperwork to report status, instead of talking to people, are known failure patterns.
- Your manager can decide against your advice. Voice your view clearly, then work to make their call succeed anyway.
- A manager may not know how the work gets done, but they must know what they want. Find out if they haven't said.
- Detail you gave in a monthly report doesn't excuse abbreviating the same detail in the yearly one.
- Solving your own problems is the job, even for a new manager who feels like everyone wants to hand theirs upward.
- Know your management's actual style, down to how they take a joke.
- If you need to understand why senior management did something, ask directly rather than assuming.
- Don't blindside your manager in the rush to get something done.
- Keep designs and plans simple. Engineers naturally over-engineer because they like a good puzzle.
