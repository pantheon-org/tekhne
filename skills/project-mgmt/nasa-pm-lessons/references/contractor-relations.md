# Contractor Relations

Paraphrased from Jerry Madden, NASA GSFC — "Lessons Learned for Project Managers" (LLIS #1956), <https://llis.nasa.gov/lesson/1956>. Consult the source for Madden's original wording and full
elaboration.

Use for vendor or contractor friction and negotiation — pick the 1-3 that fit, don't dump the list.

- Contractors respond to an attentive customer, not a micromanaging one. Don't change their plan unless it's actually flawed or too costly — better is the enemy of good.
- Know what genuinely motivates your contractor: their award structure, fiscal system, policies, and company culture.
- Contractors size up your team and staff their side of the project accordingly. A weak counterpart on your side draws their weaker people too.
- An award-fee or performance score should reflect real project and management status, cross-checked against whatever measurement system exists — consistent mismatches deserve senior attention, not
  just a shrug.
- The customer-side role is to actively drive the vendor toward a good outcome, not just monitor it from a distance. A poor result reflects on both sides, not just theirs.
- Being friendly with a vendor is fine. Becoming their friend risks your objectivity.
- Vendor staff morale shows up in the product you're buying. It's worth taking an active interest in.
- Every extra person you add on your side costs real money through the one-to-one interfaces they create with the vendor's team.
- A weak vendor-side project lead should be replaced quickly. Their job is keeping the customer satisfied on schedule, cost, and quality — not on flattery.
- Bringing a large group to a vendor visit turns it into entertainment, not business.
- Overreliance on support contractors blurs who's actually accountable for the work.
- Vendor quality varies over time. Past performance doesn't guarantee future performance, so stay vigilant rather than coasting on reputation.
- Vendors usually know your budget and intend to capture all of it. Watch their staffing closely rather than assuming restraint.
- Excessive cost-validation detail can hide the real risks. A heavily scrutinized budget can still produce a large surprise later — look at how similar past efforts actually spent, not just the
  current paper trail.
- Small, stable teams tend to reproduce good work reliably. Large teams with high staff turnover often can't — question a vendor's "track record" claim if the people who earned it aren't the people
  doing the work now.
