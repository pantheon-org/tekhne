# Managing Project Staff

Paraphrased from Jerry Madden, NASA GSFC — "Lessons Learned for Project Managers" (LLIS #1956), <https://llis.nasa.gov/lesson/1956>. Consult the source for Madden's original wording and full
elaboration.

Use for retro notes, team roster entries, and 1:1 or standup prep — pick the 1-3 that fit, don't dump the list.

- People are the root of most problems. Know your team well enough to see the real weak spots.
- Managers succeed on their staff's strength, not their own.
- Don't try to be your own systems engineer or financial manager — that's operating on yourself.
- Watch your workaholics. They can do real damage moving fast in the wrong direction, and self-imposed overload burnout is hard to spot from outside.
- Never overrule someone's work in public. Even when you direct a change, leave the implementation ownership with them.
- Your contractors and instrument teams are a deep bench of engineering talent. Use them when a problem needs attacking.
- Visit everyone building something for the project at least once. Know every manager and integration team member by name.
- Add people to a struggling effort a little at a time, the way you'd correct an under-salted dish.
- There's almost no real secret information on a project. Sharing the whole picture helps people perform better.
- Most people want to do good work. When they don't, it's usually a knowledge or expectations gap, not intent.
- A team member missing context will reasonably reach the wrong conclusion. One puzzle piece alone tells you little.
- Skipping modern tools is a mistake, but let them assist thinking rather than replace it.
- Management fundamentals don't change with the tooling. Find the right people and get out of their way.
- Only the incompetent avoid showing off their work.
- Six people makes a working meeting. Beyond that, it's information transfer, not collaboration.
- Sometimes doing nothing, or simply listening, is the most useful help you can give. If you're always solving their problems, you're effectively working for them.
- Integrity means your subordinates trust you.
- You can't watch everything, but you can watch your people. They need to know a poor job won't be accepted.
- For the rare task only one person can really do, protect and prioritize that person. A substitute often takes 2-3x longer for a worse result.
- Real recognition — a genuine say in their own piece of the work, plus acknowledgment — beats most other motivators.
- Never assume something was understood or done without asking, especially under stress, when the obvious gets skipped.
- If someone consistently won't look, ask, and analyze, help them move on to something else.
- Respect people's time. Hand out only necessary work and meetings, and shield your staff from the unnecessary ones.
- Negotiate support at the lowest level possible — as close as you can get to the person actually doing the work.
- Sometimes trusting to luck beats locking in support you already know is poor.
- A project needs a coach, not a boss, but the coach still calls some of the plays.
- Respect and a strong will get things done as well as strong-arm tactics, without the cleanup cost the latter leaves behind.
- Staff meetings: five minutes minimum, one hour maximum.
- Track how long it actually takes a decision to reach the person who has to implement it. Measure that in hours, not days.
- Let your staff talk you into a decision you'd already planned to make. The sense of having won matters to them.
- If you're the smartest person on your own project, you recruited badly.
