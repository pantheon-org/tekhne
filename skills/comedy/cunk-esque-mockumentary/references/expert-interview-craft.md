# Expert Interview Craft

This is a deep dive on writing the expert's side of the interview exchange: the half of the format that's easy to get wrong in the direction of either flattening the expert into a straight-man cardboard cutout, or tipping them into the hostility the main skill file flags as a hard anti-pattern. Use it when the interview exchange itself is the hard part of a request.

## The expert is a character too

It's tempting to treat the expert as a neutral delivery mechanism for the "correct" answer, existing only to set up the presenter's next line. That flattens the scene. The expert should read as a specific, sincere professional who has clearly answered easier questions than this one, many times, and is extending the same professional courtesy to a much harder case. Small choices: which detail they clarify first, whether they try to salvage the presenter's framing or gently discard it: give the expert a discernible personality without ever tipping into irritation.

## Question archetypes

Not every wrong question is wrong in the same way. Consider which archetype suits the moment:

- **The false-premise question**: assumes something is true that isn't, and asks about it as settled. ("So when the ice age ended, where did all the ice actually go?") The expert's job here is to gently correct the premise before answering anything.
- **The category-error question**: connects two real but unrelated things as if one obviously explains the other. ("Is a black hole basically a very committed plughole?") The expert's job is to take the comparison seriously enough to explain why it doesn't hold, without dismissing it outright.
- **The scale question**: gets a real fact right but at wildly the wrong scale or timeframe. ("So evolution: that's something that could happen to a person, say, over a long weekend?") The expert corrects the scale specifically, since that's the actual error.
- **The definitional question**: asks the expert to define a basic term in their field as though it were an unusually obscure one. ("What actually is 'evidence,' in the scientific sense?") This one works well early in an interview, since it establishes how far from the subject the presenter is starting.
- **The leading non-question**: not phrased as a question at all, but as a confident assertion inviting agreement. ("Which must have been devastating for the plates involved.") The expert has to decide whether to answer the buried question or gently redirect: either is workable.

Rotating archetypes across a longer interview (rather than using the same shape three times) keeps the exchange from feeling formulaic.

## Calibrating strained sincerity

"Strained but patient" is a range, not a fixed setting. A useful way to think about it: the expert's strain should track how many times they've now had to correct the same kind of error, not how big any single error is.

- **First correction in a scene**: warm, almost delighted: a professional getting to explain their field to someone who's asked an unusual question. Little to no visible strain.
- **Second or third correction, especially if it repeats a similar misunderstanding**: the warmth cools slightly. The expert might start pre-empting the next likely misunderstanding, which reads as strain without reading as annoyance.
- **A correction late in a longer exchange**: brief, more clipped answers are fine here: but the content should stay generous and accurate. Brevity is not the same as hostility; a short answer delivered without a jab still reads as patient.

The ceiling, regardless of how far into the exchange, is an expert who is visibly working hard to be helpful. The moment an answer reads as a jab at the presenter rather than an attempt to actually inform them, the scene has crossed into the anti-pattern the main skill file calls out.

### Worked example: strain increasing across three corrections, staying inside the ceiling

```text
EXCHANGE 1
NARRATOR: So a "fossil" is basically a rock that used to be alive?
EXPERT: Close: it's the preserved remains or trace of something that was
once alive, usually turned to stone over a very long time. Not the rock
itself waking up, as such.

EXCHANGE 2
NARRATOR: And the "very long time": are we talking, what, a decade?
EXPERT: A bit longer than that: typically tens of thousands of years at
minimum, often millions.

EXCHANGE 3
NARRATOR: So if I buried my car today, in a decade it'd basically be a fossil.
EXPERT: Not quite on that timescale, no: but I appreciate you're testing
the definition, and that's honestly a fair way to think about the boundary
cases.
```

Each answer stays accurate and generous; the third answer is visibly working a little harder to find something constructive to say, which is the strain, but it never becomes a correction delivered as a put-down.

## What sincerity is protecting

The reason hostility is banned rather than just discouraged is about where audience sympathy sits. If the expert is patient throughout, the audience's amusement stays pointed at the *gap*: the presenter's confident wrongness meeting real expertise: which is the actual joke this format is built on. If the expert gets exasperated even once, the audience's attention shifts to watching a person get annoyed at another person, which reads as conflict rather than satire, and which most viewers experience as mildly uncomfortable rather than funny, regardless of who's "right."

## Interview length and pacing

- **Single-exchange interviews** (one question, one answer) work well as a beat within a longer narration-driven segment: they should carry one clear misunderstanding and one clean correction.
- **Multi-exchange interviews** (three or more question-answer pairs) can carry their own escalation, independent of any narration around them: see the worked example above, where the escalation is in the *specificity* of the misunderstanding rather than its scale.
- Avoid interviews that go past four or five exchanges without a change of subject or a narration break: beyond that length, the expert's patience becomes the joke by default, which risks reading as exhausting rather than sustained.

## Common interview mistakes

- **The expert explains too much unprompted**: a good answer addresses exactly the misunderstanding in the question and stops: a long, complete lecture reads as the writer explaining the joke rather than the expert answering in character.
- **The expert's correction is actually a joke at the presenter's expense**: even a gentle "no, that's not remotely right" delivered with a raised eyebrow (in prose, phrasing that reads as pointed) tips toward hostility. Keep corrections addressed to the *idea*, not the presenter's competence.
- **The presenter's question is too obviously a joke**: if the misunderstanding is so large it reads as a scripted bit rather than something a real, if clueless, person might actually ask, the expert's sincere answer stops making sense as a reaction. The best questions are the ones that sound almost reasonable for one beat before the wrongness lands.
- **Treating the expert as interchangeable across scenes**: a historian, a physicist, and a civil engineer should sound like specialists in different fields, with different things that visibly matter to them, even when the underlying strained-sincerity register is the same.

## Field-specific flavour for the expert's answers

The expert archetype stays constant (patient, sincere, professionally generous) but what they visibly care about should shift with their field. A few starting points:

- **Historians** tend to care about sourcing and certainty: their answers often qualify how *confident* the historical record actually is, which gives them a natural way to gently push back on the presenter's flat certainty without ever sounding annoyed.
- **Scientists** (physical or life sciences) tend to care about scale, mechanism, and the difference between correlation and cause: their corrections often work by isolating the one specific number, unit, or mechanism the presenter got wrong, rather than rejecting the whole claim.
- **Civil or structural specialists** tend to care about how something was actually built or maintained: their answers often ground a wildly wrong claim by describing the real, unglamorous process involved, which produces a nice contrast with the presenter's more dramatic framing.
- **Art or cultural specialists** tend to care about context and intent: their answers often explain what a work or tradition meant to the people who made it, which gives them a natural way to correct a presenter who's read a work purely at face value.

None of this is a rule the expert must follow; it's a way to make four different interview scenes in four different fields feel like four different people, rather than one "generic expert" reused with a different job title.

## Writing the presenter's follow-up after a correction

How the presenter responds to being corrected matters almost as much as the original question. Two reliable options:

- **Absorb and restate wrong**: the presenter accepts the correction, then immediately restates it back in a way that's subtly wrong again, treating their own restatement as though it were simply a paraphrase. This keeps the exchange moving and gives the expert something new to (patiently) correct.
- **Absorb and pivot**: the presenter accepts the correction at face value, then asks a new, differently wrong question that seems to follow logically from what the expert just said, but doesn't actually follow at all.

What doesn't work is the presenter simply repeating their original wrong claim unchanged after being corrected: that reads as the presenter not listening, which shifts the joke from "sincere misunderstanding" to "not paying attention," a flatter and less interesting failure mode.

## A short reference glossary of interview beats

- **Set-up question**: the first question in an exchange, establishing the presenter's angle on the subject. Usually the false-premise or definitional archetype.
- **Correction**: the expert's first response, addressing the premise before (if at all) answering anything further.
- **Restatement**: the presenter's response to the correction, either absorb-and-restate-wrong or absorb-and-pivot.
- **Second correction**: the expert's response to the restatement, calibrated slightly more strained than the first correction per the scale described above.
- **Exit line**: either party can close the exchange; a presenter exit line often pivots back toward narration ("Fascinating. Anyway, moving on..."), while an expert exit line often lands on a patient, slightly rueful summary of the whole exchange.

Not every interview needs all five beats: a single set-up-and-correction pair is a complete, usable exchange on its own. The glossary is here to name the parts when a longer exchange needs more structure than "question, answer, question, answer."
