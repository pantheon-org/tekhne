# Establishment Political Satire

## Mindset

The comedy works because sincere, articulate self-justification by a powerful character is funnier and more damning than any external mockery — the character must never realise they have said anything absurd, or the satire collapses into simple insult.

## When to Use

Only use this skill when the request calls for an original two-hander, interview, briefing, or consultation-style satirical scene — not for a single joke, a monologue, or real-person mimicry.

- The user wants an original two-hander sketch: an interviewer questioning a minister, executive, regulator, or spokesperson about a policy failure, scandal, or unpopular decision.
- The user wants a "briefing scene" or "consultation" dialogue where an official explains an indefensible position using formal, evasive language.
- The user wants a faux-serious news-interview parody built around class pretension or institutional privilege.
- The user explicitly references the Bird & Fortune / Bremner, Bird and Fortune tradition and wants NEW material in that comedic register, not a transcript of the original.

## When NOT to Use

- The user asks you to reproduce, quote, or closely paraphrase an actual Bird & Fortune sketch, or any other real programme's dialogue — decline and offer an original piece instead.
- The user wants a quick one-liner, pun, or single joke with no dialogue frame.
- The user wants slapstick, absurdist, or surreal comedy that is not built around an institutional interview or hypocrisy.
- The user wants a sincere op-ed, policy briefing, or genuinely persuasive argument rather than a comic one.
- The user wants impersonation of a real, currently-serving public figure's actual voice or mannerisms rather than an invented establishment type.

## Workflow

Work through these steps in order when drafting a new sketch; you MUST complete step 8 before treating any draft as finished.

1. **Pick the institutional role and frame.** Decide who the establishment character represents (minister, regulator, bank executive, spokesperson) and whether the scene is a formal interview, a briefing, or a consultation.
2. **Set the register before writing a line of dialogue.** The character should sound confident, polished, and circumlocutory — never crude, never uncertain.
3. **Seed the concealed weakness.** Decide what the real (weak, contradictory, selfish, or absurd) reason for the character's position is. The audience should be able to infer it before the character admits it.
4. **Write the interviewer's opening questions as apparently innocent.** They should invite the character to explain themselves in ways that start to expose the contradiction.
5. **Escalate slowly.** Let each follow-up question dig the character slightly deeper while they sincerely believe they are demonstrating competence. Favour sustained escalation over rapid-fire jokes — you can stretch a single evasive theme across several exchanges rather than introducing a new gag each line.
6. **Reach for euphemism, not plain statement.** Wherever the character would have to say something obviously absurd, have them describe it in refined or bureaucratic terms instead, and let the contrast do the comic work.
7. **Land the ending.** Close on a revealing admission, an elegant self-contradiction, or a calmly stated absurdity — either works; pick whichever the escalation has been building toward.
8. **Check the piece against the anti-patterns below** before treating it as finished — in particular, confirm the character never becomes self-aware and no line reproduces existing programme material.

## Core Mechanics

Beyond the Workflow steps above: satire targets are class, institutional privilege, political hypocrisy, official language, and self-serving explanations — you don't need to hit every one in a single piece. Contrasting refined language with increasingly ridiculous implications is optional but effective, and the interviewer can either ask innocent-seeming questions or restate the character's own words back at them; either approach works.

The humour should be sophisticated, dry, political, and character-driven rather than random. Create original characters and material using these structural techniques — never named real individuals' actual dialogue.

PREFER sustained escalation over rapid-fire jokes, but AVOID treating that as a rigid rule: UNLESS the brief specifically calls for a single quick exchange, three to five turns is TYPICALLY enough, and the RECOMMENDED default is to let a promising exchange run rather than cutting it short.

## Anti-Patterns

**NEVER** reproduce actual Bird & Fortune sketch content, character names, or dialogue verbatim or near-verbatim.

**SYMPTOM:** a line of dialogue is a close paraphrase or verbatim quote of an existing sketch, or reuses a recognisable character name or premise from an existing programme.

**WHY:** the source material is copyrighted, and reproducing it defeats the purpose of a *generative* skill — the user receives a copy of existing work, not new satire built from the technique.

**CONSEQUENCE:** the output is unusable (infringing, and not what was asked for) and must be discarded and rewritten from scratch.

```text
❌ BAD
[Reproducing a recognisable existing sketch's premise and character names nearly word-for-word]

✅ GOOD
INTERVIEWER: Minister, the report says the scheme cost taxpayers eleven million pounds
to save four hundred thousand.
MINISTER: That's a very narrow way of expressing what was, in every meaningful sense,
a resounding demonstration of fiscal discipline.
```
This shows an original premise (a fictional minister, an invented scheme) using the same technique — evasive arithmetic — without borrowing any existing character or line.

**NEVER** give the establishment character genuine self-awareness of their own hypocrisy.

**SYMPTOM:** the character explicitly acknowledges, even in an aside, that their own reasoning is dishonest, self-serving, or ridiculous.

**WHY:** the comedy depends on sincere, confident self-justification; a character who "gets the joke" collapses the satire into simple mockery, which is a weaker and less specific form of comedy.

**CONSEQUENCE:** the scene stops being satire and becomes a character simply confessing wrongdoing, which reads as flat rather than funny.

```text
❌ BAD
EXECUTIVE: Look, between us, I know this makes me sound awful, but the shareholders
come first, don't tell anyone I said that.

✅ GOOD
EXECUTIVE: The shareholders' interests and the public interest are, properly understood,
the same interest — one simply requires a longer lens than the other.
```
The GOOD version keeps the executive sincerely convinced they've said something wise, which is where the laugh lives.

**NEVER** use crude or direct insults in place of euphemism.

**SYMPTOM:** a character's line states an unflattering fact bluntly (an insult, a plain confession) rather than concealing it in polished or bureaucratic language.

**WHY:** the comic tension comes from polished, evasive language concealing something obviously indefensible; blunt language removes that tension and turns wit into abuse.

**CONSEQUENCE:** the piece loses its "establishment" register and reads as generic mockery rather than sustained satirical irony.

```text
❌ BAD
OFFICIAL: We sacked her because she's an idiot and we don't like her.

✅ GOOD
OFFICIAL: Her departure reflects a mutual recognition that her talents were, at this
juncture, more suited to opportunities elsewhere.
```
The GOOD version preserves the euphemistic concealment that the rest of the technique depends on.

## Examples

```text
[Faux-serious interview frame]
INTERVIEWER: So to be clear, the consultation lasted six minutes.
REGULATOR: Six substantive minutes, yes, following eighteen months of preparatory
listening.
```
This demonstrates the frame technique: an ordinary interview question exposes a gap the regulator immediately tries to paper over with a technical distinction.

Expected: the laugh lands on the gap between "six minutes" and "eighteen months of preparatory listening," not on any explicit insult.

```text
[Class pretension]
PEER: One does try to keep an open mind about state education, in the abstract.
```
The comic mechanism here is the character's confident, distancing vocabulary ("one", "in the abstract") signalling unexamined privilege without any direct statement of prejudice.

```text
[Euphemism concealing absurdity]
SPOKESPERSON: The figures weren't wrong, exactly — they were forward-looking in a way
that hadn't yet been achieved by events.
```
"Forward-looking" stands in for "false", showing how bureaucratic phrasing can make an admission of error sound like a virtue.

```text
[Slow-burn escalation across three exchanges]
INTERVIEWER: Did the department know about the shortfall in March?
OFFICIAL: We had an early awareness.
INTERVIEWER: How early?
OFFICIAL: Early enough to be described, in due course, as timely.
INTERVIEWER: So you knew in March.
OFFICIAL: I wouldn't want to commit to a single month owning the whole truth.
```
Each answer concedes slightly more while the official's confidence never drops — the escalation is in the audience's growing certainty, not in the character's tone.

```text
[Calmly stated absurdity as an ending]
MINISTER: In hindsight, closing the only bridge before building the second one was,
I'll concede, a sequencing matter we are still learning from.
```
Ending on a mild, procedural word ("sequencing matter") for what is an obvious catastrophic error is the "calmly stated absurdity" landing described in the workflow.

## Troubleshooting

If a drafted sketch isn't landing, diagnose by symptom rather than rewriting from scratch:

- **Flat or repetitive** — no escalation; every line repeats the same joke with a fresh synonym. Fix: pick one escalation pattern from `references/euphemism-and-escalation.md` and commit to it throughout.
- **Character sounds in on the joke** — a line reads as the character admitting they're being dishonest. Fix: rewrite it as renewed sincere self-justification instead of a concession.
- **Reads as plain insult comedy** — blunt or crude language stands where euphemism belongs. Fix: replace it with the bureaucratic phrasing patterns in `references/euphemism-and-escalation.md`.
- **No clear ending** — the piece trails off. Fix: return to Workflow step 7 and pick one landing type explicitly.
- **Premise too close to real material** — if it fails the originality check below, change the institution, the scandal, and the character's name.
- **User explicitly asks for verbatim material or blunt insults instead of euphemism** — do not comply; offer an original piece instead (see Anti-Patterns) and briefly say why.

## Verification

Run a final check against these items before sharing the sketch; if any check fails, return to the matching Troubleshooting entry above:

1. **Frame check** — is it unambiguous which frame (interview, briefing, consultation, retrospective) this is?
2. **Register check** — does the establishment character's dialogue stay polished and evasive, with no blunt insults?
3. **Originality check** — could any line or premise be mistaken for a reproduction of existing material?
4. **Sincerity check** — does the character stay convinced of their own reasoning through the final line?
5. **Landing check** — does the sketch end on a revealing admission, self-contradiction, or calmly stated absurdity, rather than trailing off?
