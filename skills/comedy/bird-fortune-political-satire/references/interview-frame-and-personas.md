# Interview Frame and Persona Construction: A Deep Dive

This reference expands on Workflow steps 1 and 2 from the main skill: choosing the institutional frame and setting the establishment character's register before writing any dialogue. Getting this right up front avoids the most common failure mode — a sketch that reads as generic argument rather than a specific satirical form.

## Choosing the frame

The frame is the pretext under which the two characters are talking. It matters because it sets what the interviewer is allowed to ask and what the establishment character is expected to perform. Four reliable frames, with what each one is good for:

1. **The formal interview** — a journalist or broadcaster questioning an official about a specific, already-public failure. Best when the sketch is built around a known-shape scandal (cost overrun, safety failure, broken promise) and you want the interviewer to have documentary-style leverage: numbers, dates, prior quotes.
2. **The briefing** — the establishment character is explaining a policy or decision to an audience assumed to be sympathetic (colleagues, a select committee, the press pool), and the interviewer figure is more of a skeptical questioner within that room. This frame suits material where the absurdity is in the policy's internal logic rather than in a single incident.
3. **The consultation** — the establishment character is nominally seeking input but has already decided the outcome. This frame is well suited to satirising box-ticking process ("we've consulted widely") and works even with only one line of dialogue from the second character, since the joke can be in how little the consultation actually consults.
4. **The retrospective / inquiry** — the establishment character is answering for something now conceded to have gone wrong, in a setting (inquiry panel, post-mortem review) explicitly designed to establish fault. This frame is the strongest choice when you want the ending to land as a "revealing admission," because the setting already expects one.

Pick the frame before drafting dialogue; it determines what kind of questions are plausible and what kind of evasions read as in-character rather than random.

## Fixing the character's institutional role

Before writing lines, decide:

- **What institution or sector** the character represents (a government department, a regulator, a bank, a utility, a professional body, a royal-adjacent institution). Different institutions carry different default vocabularies — regulators reach for "proportionate," utilities reach for "resilience," banks reach for "prudent."
- **What the character is personally protecting** — their own competence, their department's budget, a personal relationship with a donor or patron, their social standing. This is the "concealed weakness" the Workflow tells you to seed in step 3, and it should be specific enough that you could state it in one sentence even though the character never will.
- **What the character has convinced themselves is true.** This is not the same as what they're protecting. A minister protecting their department's reputation might have genuinely convinced themselves that the department behaved well; that sincere self-belief is what keeps them from becoming self-aware (the anti-pattern).

## Setting the register

Establishment characters in this tradition share a register, but it is not one uniform "posh voice." Useful register dials, each independently adjustable:

- **Formality of address** — does the character use full titles, refer to themselves in the passive, use "one" instead of "I"? Higher formality suits older, more traditional institutions (peers, senior civil servants); lower formality but equal evasiveness suits modern corporate spokespeople ("look, honestly, from where we're sitting...").
- **Technical jargon density** — regulators and executives can hide behind genuine technical vocabulary (a compliance framework, a risk appetite statement) where the joke is that the jargon is both real and being misused to dodge the question.
- **Warmth versus froideur** — some characters are comic because they're icy and precise; others because they're falsely matey with the interviewer ("look, between friends...") while still being completely evasive. Either works; pick one and hold it, since switching registers mid-sketch usually reads as the writer losing control of the character rather than as a deliberate effect.
- **Confidence trajectory** — decide up front whether the character's confidence stays flat throughout (recommended for shorter sketches, see the euphemism-and-escalation reference's "concession creep" pattern) or visibly strains near the end without ever fully breaking (suited to longer sketches building toward a bigger admission).

## Constructing the interviewer

The interviewer is not a straight man in the sense of being unfunny — their questions are doing real comic work by being precise, calm, and apparently reasonable. A few reusable interviewer techniques:

- **Quote the character's own words back to them.** "You said, in March, that this was fully funded." This is more effective than the interviewer stating an opinion, because it makes the establishment character responsible for reconciling their own contradiction rather than defending against an accusation.
- **Ask for a number.** Vague evasive language collapses fastest when asked to become a specific figure, date, or name. "How many, precisely?" is a strong beat because any answer (a real number, a refusal, a vaguer number) is comic.
- **Restate the euphemism in plain terms and wait.** "So — to be clear — nobody checked." Letting the plain restatement sit, unembellished, for a beat before the character responds is often funnier than the interviewer adding their own commentary.
- **Avoid editorialising.** The interviewer should almost never call the character a liar, hypocrite, or fool directly — the audience should reach that conclusion from the character's own words, not be told to by the interviewer. An interviewer who editorialises is doing the audience's work for them and usually weakens the sketch.

## A short worked example of frame + persona choices (original)

Frame: retrospective inquiry. Institution: a regional transport authority. Protecting: the character's own decision, made two years earlier, to defer maintenance spending. Sincere self-belief: that deferring the spending was a legitimate judgement call given competing priorities, not negligence. Register: high formality, heavy technical jargon, flat confidence throughout, breaking only in the final line.

```text
PANEL: The report finds the delay was a direct contributor to the incident.
DIRECTOR: The report identifies a contributing factor among several, within a
multi-variate risk environment.
PANEL: Was funding available at the time?
DIRECTOR: Funding was available in the sense that funds exist; whether it was
available for this specific line item is a question of prioritisation, and
prioritisation is, by definition, a discretionary exercise.
PANEL: So you chose not to spend it.
DIRECTOR: I exercised the discretion the role affords me. That the discretion is now
being characterised as a choice is, I think, slightly unfair to the concept of
discretion.
```

This demonstrates the frame (inquiry panel expecting accountability), the persona (technical jargon, protecting a past discretionary decision, sincerely defending the *concept* of discretion rather than admitting the outcome), and a flat-confidence escalation pattern building to a mildly absurd final defence of an abstract noun.

## Matching frame to sketch length

Not every frame suits every length of piece. As a rough guide:

- A **consultation** frame can work in as few as two or three lines, since the joke ("we consulted, but decided beforehand") can land almost immediately.
- A **formal interview** typically needs four to six exchanges to let the concession-creep or escalating-justification patterns build properly (see the euphemism-and-escalation reference).
- A **briefing** suits a slightly longer piece, since it often has room for one or two audience/questioner interjections rather than a continuous back-and-forth.
- A **retrospective/inquiry** frame rewards the longest pieces, because the setting already implies a documented history the interviewer can draw on across many turns.

If a sketch feels rushed, consider whether the chosen frame actually needs more room than the current draft gives it, rather than cutting the piece shorter.

## Two persona archetypes worth distinguishing

Within "establishment character," two broad archetypes recur, and it's worth deciding which one you're writing:

1. **The true believer.** This character has genuinely convinced themselves that their position is correct and defensible. Their evasions are not conscious tactics — they experience the interviewer's questions as slightly unfair or as missing the point. This archetype suits the "sincere self-belief" requirement most naturally and is the safer default.
2. **The practised operator.** This character knows, at some level, how to handle difficult questions — they have media training, a prepared line, a rehearsed calm. The key distinction from the anti-pattern is that they still believe the *substance* of what they're saying, even if they're also aware they're handling the interview skilfully. They can be pleased with their own performance without being aware that the underlying position is absurd. This archetype adds a layer of comic irony (the audience watches technique as well as content) but is slightly harder to keep on the right side of the self-awareness anti-pattern, since "pleased with handling the question well" can tip into "aware the answer itself is ridiculous" if you're not careful.

Either archetype is usable; the true believer is the more reliable choice for shorter or first-draft pieces.

## The interviewer's own possible flaws

The interviewer does not need to be a neutral cipher. Some options that keep them interesting without editorialising:

- **The overly precise interviewer** — someone who seems almost pedantic about getting an exact figure or date, which is itself mildly funny while still doing real comic work by cornering the establishment character.
- **The falsely deferential interviewer** — someone who prefaces hard questions with excessive politeness ("I hope you won't mind my asking..."), which contrasts with how pointed the actual question is.
- **The tired interviewer** — someone who has clearly heard this kind of evasion many times before and asks the follow-up with weary familiarity rather than surprise, which can undercut the establishment character's assumption that they're getting away with something novel.

Avoid an interviewer who is visibly angry, sarcastic, or mocking — that tips the interviewer into editorialising and does the audience's judgement for them, which the main skill's interviewer-construction guidance advises against.

## Checklist before writing dialogue

- Can you state the frame in one sentence, and does every line of dialogue make sense within it?
- Can you state what the character is protecting and what they sincerely believe, and are those two things different?
- Have you picked one confidence trajectory (flat, or straining-but-unbroken) rather than letting it drift?
- Does the interviewer ever editorialise? If so, consider cutting the editorial line and trusting the character's own words to carry it.
- Have you chosen (even implicitly) whether the character is a true believer or a practised operator, and does every line stay consistent with that choice?
- Does the frame you picked suit the length of piece you're writing, or does it need more room to build?
