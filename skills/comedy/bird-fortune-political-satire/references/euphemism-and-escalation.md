# Euphemism and Escalation: A Deep Dive

This reference expands on two of the core mechanics from the main skill: how to build euphemistic language that conceals absurdity convincingly, and how to pace escalation across a sketch so the comedy compounds rather than spikes and flattens.

## Why euphemism carries the weight

In this comedic tradition, the joke is never the underlying fact (a lie, a failure, a self-serving decision). The joke is the *distance* between that fact and the language chosen to describe it. The bigger the gap, and the more confidently the character strides across it, the funnier the line. This means the craft is almost entirely in word choice, not in plot.

A useful test: if you can imagine the euphemism appearing, unironically, in an actual press release, annual report, or government statement, it is doing its job. If it sounds like a joke a comedy writer invented, it is too knowing and will read as the character being self-aware (an anti-pattern — see the main SKILL.md).

## A working vocabulary of concealment

Consider these families of euphemistic substitution. You do not need to use these exact words — invent your own for each sketch — but the categories are reusable:

1. **Temporal softening** — turning "we knew and did nothing" into "we were monitoring the situation with appropriate urgency." The absurdity is in stretching the definition of "urgency" rather than denying the timeline outright.
2. **Scale minimisation** — turning a large failure into a "learning opportunity," a "adjustment," or an "isolated recalibration." The character should treat the scale of the word as self-evidently proportionate to the scale of the disaster, which it obviously is not.
3. **Agency laundering** — removing the subject from the sentence. "Mistakes were made" is the classic form; more sophisticated versions attribute the failure to abstract forces ("market conditions," "legacy systems," "the pace of change") rather than to any person's decision.
4. **Virtue reframing** — describing a self-serving act using the vocabulary of duty or sacrifice. A pay rise becomes "recognising the burden of leadership"; a resignation with a payout becomes "stepping back to allow the organisation to heal."
5. **Procedural distancing** — retreating into process language when substance is indefensible. "That's a matter for the appropriate committee," or "I wouldn't want to pre-empt the review," lets a character avoid answering while sounding scrupulous rather than evasive.

## Building euphemism into a line

A reliable construction pattern:

```text
[ordinary/blunt fact] --> [character's euphemism] --> [interviewer restates the blunt fact] --> [character defends the euphemism as more accurate]
```

Example skeleton (original, invented):

```text
INTERVIEWER: You closed the helpline.
OFFICIAL: We consolidated the customer journey into channels better suited to modern
expectations.
INTERVIEWER: There's no phone number any more.
OFFICIAL: There's no phone number, no. But there is, I'd stress, a very rich digital
experience.
```

Notice the official never denies the fact. Denial would make them a liar, which is a different (and cruder) kind of character. Reframing the fact while conceding it is what keeps the character sincere and the satire sharp.

## Pacing escalation

Escalation is the mechanism by which a sketch builds rather than just repeats the same joke. Three approaches, roughly in order of subtlety:

### 1. Concession creep

Each answer concedes a little more ground than the last, but the character's confidence never wavers. The audience's certainty rises steadily while the character's tone stays flat. This is the most reliable pattern for a two-to-four exchange sketch, and it is the one demonstrated in the main SKILL.md's "slow-burn escalation" example.

### 2. Escalating absurdity of justification

Rather than conceding more fact, the character's *justifications* become progressively more elaborate and further from plausible, while the underlying fact stays fixed. This works well when the fact itself is already fully exposed early and the comedy is entirely in watching the character's rhetorical resources run out.

```text
INTERVIEWER: The bridge collapsed six weeks after it opened.
ENGINEER: An entirely foreseeable stress-testing phase, yes.
INTERVIEWER: Nobody tested it before people were using it.
ENGINEER: The public were, in effect, invited to participate in the testing phase.
INTERVIEWER: As passengers.
ENGINEER: As stakeholders.
```

### 3. Escalating stakes

The interviewer's questions move outward in scope — from the specific incident, to who knew, to who else is affected, to what happens next — forcing the character to defend an ever-widening claim with the same tone they used for the narrow one. This suits longer sketches (five or more exchanges) where you want the character's composure to look increasingly untenable relative to the growing scale of what they're defending.

## When escalation is happening too fast (or not at all)

If a sketch feels flat, it is usually one of two failures:

- **No escalation at all** — every exchange makes the same joke about the same fact with a fresh synonym. Fix by choosing one of the three patterns above and committing to a direction (more concession, more elaborate justification, or wider stakes) across the whole sketch.
- **Escalation collapsing too early** — the character admits the whole truth by the second line, leaving nowhere to go. Fix by holding back the full admission for the ending (see the main skill's "Land the ending" workflow step) and using the middle exchanges to concede only partial ground each time.

## Matching euphemism register to institution type

Different institutions default to different euphemistic vocabularies, and matching the register to the institution makes the concealment more convincing:

- **Government departments and ministers** reach for words like "ongoing review," "lessons learned," "in the round," and "at this point in time." The comedy is often in how these phrases can stretch to cover almost any timeframe or failure.
- **Regulators** reach for "proportionate," "risk-based approach," and "engagement with stakeholders." Their euphemisms tend to reframe inaction as a considered methodology.
- **Banks and financial institutions** reach for "prudent," "de-risking," and "capital discipline." Their euphemisms often reframe self-interest as caution on behalf of others (depositors, shareholders, the wider economy).
- **Utilities and infrastructure operators** reach for "resilience," "investment programme," and "network conditions." Their euphemisms tend to reframe underinvestment as an engineering inevitability.
- **Corporate spokespeople** reach for "customer journey," "aligning with our values," and "moving forward." Their euphemisms often reframe a retreat as a strategic choice.

Invent your own specific phrases within these families rather than reusing the examples verbatim — the point is the pattern, not a fixed script.

## Common failure modes in euphemism writing

- **The euphemism is too on-the-nose.** If a euphemism is obviously a joke rather than something a real institution might say, it breaks the illusion of sincerity. "We're going through a period of executive vanishing" reads as a writer's joke; "we're managing a period of leadership transition" reads as something an institution would actually publish, and is funnier for it precisely because the audience has to do the translation themselves.
- **The euphemism does all the work alone.** A single good euphemistic phrase, without an interviewer to press on it or a follow-up that makes the gap between language and fact explicit, can land as merely clever rather than funny. Pair every strong euphemism with a follow-up question or restatement that forces the character to either repeat it more confidently or reach for a second one.
- **The character over-explains the euphemism.** If the character immediately unpacks their own phrase ("what I mean by that is..."), they've done the audience's interpretive work for them and removed the joke. Let the euphemism sit, and let the interviewer (or the audience) draw the plain conclusion.

## Checklist before finishing a sketch

- Does every euphemism plausibly belong in a real press statement, minus the specific absurd content?
- Could you delete any middle exchange without losing information? If so, the escalation isn't doing enough per line.
- Does the character's confidence stay constant even as what they're forced to concede grows? A dip in confidence usually signals the character has become self-aware, which is the anti-pattern to avoid.
- Does the euphemism's register match the institution (a bank should not sound like a government department, and vice versa)?
- Is there at least one follow-up question or restatement that presses on each key euphemism, rather than leaving it unchallenged?
