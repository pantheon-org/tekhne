# Provocateur Stand-up Craft

A deep dive on the direct-address stand-up mode of this skill: a blunt,
taboo-puncturing statement delivered without cushioning. Use this when a request
wants a monologue-style bit, a roast line, or a short run of stand-up material —
for a full scripted scene with a character and onlookers, see
`character-cringe-craft.md` instead.

## The Core Engine: Removing the Cushion

Ordinary polite conversation surrounds an uncomfortable truth with softening
material: qualifiers, disclaimers, tone signals that say "I know this is a lot, but
—". This mode's entire mechanism is removing that cushioning and delivering the
claim as if it were the most obvious, unremarkable statement in the world. The
laugh comes from the gap between how loaded the subject is and how unbothered the
delivery is. The moment any cushioning returns — a hedge, an apology, a "no offence
but" — the gap collapses and the bit becomes an opinion piece instead of a joke.

## Selecting the Taboo Target

Not every uncomfortable subject works equally well. Strong targets share three
properties:

1. **Near-universal private awareness.** Most of the audience has privately
   thought the thing, or something adjacent to it, and is relieved (and a little
   scandalised) to hear it said aloud.
2. **A social script everyone knows and avoids breaking.** Death, illness,
   religion, family, workplace hierarchy, celebrity, and identity all carry
   well-known scripts for "the correct thing to say" — which means there is an
   obvious wrong thing to say instead, and the audience recognises exactly which
   rule is being broken.
3. **Enough specificity to be a claim, not a category.** "Funerals are weird" is a
   category. "Nobody at a funeral has ever meant a single word of 'they're in a
   better place'" is a claim — falsifiable-sounding, specific, and therefore
   funnier than the vague category it's drawn from.

## Constructing the Blunt Opening Line

A useful structural test: the line should survive being read in a flat, bored
voice and still land. That means the humour has to be in the claim itself, not in
vocal performance layered on top.

Mechanically:

- **One sentence, one breath.** If it needs a comma to set up a second clause of
  qualification, that second clause is probably a hedge — cut it.
- **No softening words.** Strike "kind of," "I mean," "not to be that person, but,"
  "look, I'm just saying" from the opening line specifically (they're fine later,
  in a different register, but not attached to the claim itself).
- **State it as established fact, not opinion.** "I think funerals are mostly
  performance" invites debate. "Funerals are mostly performance" invites a laugh
  because it refuses to invite debate.

```text
HEDGED (weak):
I don't know, maybe this is just me, but I sort of feel like
weddings are kind of a lot of money for one day?

UNHEDGED (the target shape):
Weddings are a down payment on a divorce you haven't had yet.
```
The unhedged version removes every softener and states the claim as settled fact,
which is what makes it land as a joke rather than a complaint.

## The Escalation Follow-up

The line immediately after the blunt statement must make things worse, not
better. "Worse" can mean:

- **Personalising it** — applying the general claim to a specific, uncomfortable
  instance.
- **Extending the logic further than the audience expected** — following the
  claim's implication one step past where politeness would have stopped.
- **Removing the last possible excuse** — closing off the escape route a listener
  might have been quietly holding onto ("at least it's not X").

```text
LINE: Nobody at a funeral has ever meant a single word of "they're
in a better place."

ESCALATION (worsens, doesn't soften):
The only place anyone in that room actually thinks he is, is dead,
and the only reason we're all standing here in matching shoes is
so nobody has to say that part out loud.

WEAK ALTERNATIVE (softens instead of escalating):
But obviously grief is complicated and everyone copes differently,
so no judgement either way.
```
The weak alternative reintroduces the cushioning the opening line just removed —
it is the exact failure mode this section exists to prevent.

## Mock Self-Congratulation

A recurring structural move: immediately before or after the taboo line, have the
narrator praise their own open-mindedness, honesty, or tolerance — right at the
point where they are demonstrating something closer to the opposite. This works
because it mimics a real conversational tic (people who preface bluntness with
claims about their own bluntness) and exaggerates it.

```text
I'm probably the most tolerant person you'll ever meet, which is
why I can say with total confidence that your uncle's "spiritual
journey" is a midlife crisis with worse Wi-Fi.
```
The self-praise ("most tolerant person") sits directly next to the least tolerant
possible follow-through, and the line depends on that juxtaposition rather than on
the insult alone.

## Flippant Tone for Solemn Subjects

Choose a subject the audience expects to be treated with weight (illness, death,
grief, tragedy) and describe it in flat, administrative, or transactional language
— the register you'd use for scheduling a meeting, not for a eulogy. The mismatch
between register and subject is the joke; the content of the claim can be
relatively mild if the tonal mismatch is sharp enough.

```text
My grandmother's funeral ran forty minutes over, which — for a
woman who was on time for absolutely nothing else in ninety-one
years — feels, if anything, on brand.
```
Nothing here is shocking in content; the flippant, logistics-report framing of a
death ("ran forty minutes over") against the genuine affection in "on brand" is
what creates the discomfort-laugh combination.

## Escalation Toward the Gut-Punch Closer

For a longer bit (several lines rather than one), structure it as: opening blunt
claim, one or two lines of supporting material that reinforce the claim from a
different angle, then a closer that refuses the audience a comfortable exit —
often by pointing the claim back at the audience or narrator rather than leaving it
safely external.

Consider ending on the line that would be hardest to applaud politely, rather than
the line that resolves the tension — resolution is the softening this mode is
built to avoid.

## Pacing and Silence

In performance this mode leans on pauses after the blunt line, before the
escalation, to let the audience sit in the discomfort. In written form, represent
this with a line break, an em-dash cut short, or a short stage direction like
`[beat]` rather than more words — adding words to fill the pause usually reads as
nervous over-explaining, which undercuts the bluntness.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Hedging immediately after the line.** Any reassurance, disclaimer, or "but
  seriously" placed right after the taboo statement removes the nerve that made it
  land.
- **Over-justifying why it's okay to say.** A long explanation of the narrator's
  standing to make the joke (family history, personal experience) can work as
  setup *before* the line, but placed after, it reads as apologising with extra
  steps.
- **Shock without escalating logic.** Simply raising the volume or crudeness of
  the language without extending the claim's logic numbs the audience instead of
  building the bit — escalation should sharpen the point, not just get louder.
- **Turning the claim into a debate.** Adding "some people would say," "I could be
  wrong," or a rebuttal to an imagined critic reframes the bit as an argument,
  which invites the audience to evaluate it rather than laugh at it.

## Worked Example Walkthrough

A short original bit assembled from the pieces above (invented for this skill, not
drawn from any existing act):

```text
Everyone says they want to die peacefully in their sleep, surrounded
by family. Nobody wants that. What you actually want is to die
peacefully in your sleep alone, so nobody has to see your face doing
whatever it does at the end, and your family finds out on a phone
call and gets to keep the memory of you looking fine.

I'm not being morbid — I'm the only one in this room being honest
about death, which, frankly, given how much everyone claims to love
their families, is a strange thing for me to be first at.

[beat]

You'll all go home tonight and not mention this to anyone, and that
silence is the whole point I just made, happening in real time.
```

Notes on why this works: the opening line states a settled-fact claim with no
hedge; the second line is the mock self-congratulation beat ("the only one being
honest"); the closer escalates by turning the claim back on the room itself rather
than resolving it, and the `[beat]` marks the pause instead of adding words to fill
it.
