# Character Cringe Craft

A deep dive on the character-based mode of this skill: an oblivious, self-important
figure surrounded by embarrassed onlookers. Use this when a request needs a full
multi-beat scene (a sketch, a mockumentary segment, a script excerpt) rather than a
single blunt line — that single-line case belongs in `provocateur-standup-craft.md`
instead.

## The Core Engine: Self-Image vs Reality Gap

The comedy in this mode is not the misstep itself. It is the distance between two
things that the audience can see and the character cannot: what the character
believes they just demonstrated (open-mindedness, generosity, wit, authority) and
what they actually demonstrated (the opposite). The joke lives entirely in that gap.
The moment the character notices the gap, it closes, and the scene stops being
funny and starts being drama or apology. Every choice below exists to keep the gap
open as long as possible.

## Building the Oblivious Character

Three ingredients make the character work:

1. **A status position that should confer self-awareness but doesn't.** Managers,
   minor celebrities, hosts, elder relatives at a lectern — someone whose role
   implies they ought to know better, so the failure reads as a failure of
   self-knowledge, not just bad luck.
2. **A specific, nameable delusion.** Not "he's a bad person" but a precise claim
   the character holds about themselves: *I am the cool boss*, *I am colour-blind
   about race*, *I am great with the graveyard-shift staff*, *I am a natural at
   eulogies*. The more specific the delusion, the more precisely it can be
   contradicted.
3. **A verbal tic the character thinks is charming.** A catchphrase, a habit of
   asking "no offence, but—" before something offensive, a reflex to explain a joke
   immediately after telling it. This tic is reusable across beats and gives
   onlookers something concrete to react to.

Consider (not require) giving the character one genuine positive trait — competence
at the actual job, real affection for a colleague — so the audience's discomfort
comes with a flicker of sympathy rather than pure contempt. A character who is
wrong about *everything* reads as a cartoon; a character who is right about most
things and catastrophically wrong about self-perception reads as a person.

```text
BEFORE (flat, no gap):
MANAGER: I don't care what anyone thinks of me.

AFTER (specific delusion the audience can see through):
MANAGER: I'm not one of those bosses who needs to be liked. Everyone
likes me anyway, obviously, because I'm hilarious and completely
without ego. That's just a fact about me.
```
The AFTER version works because the claim ("without ego") is disproved by the
sentence containing it.

## The Onlooker Function

Onlookers are not a Greek chorus explaining the joke — the moment an onlooker says
"that's inappropriate," the audience stops doing the work themselves and the scene
flattens. Their job is to register the gap through restraint:

- **Silence** — the strongest tool. A beat where nobody responds does more than a
  line of dialogue.
- **Displaced attention** — someone suddenly very interested in their coffee, their
  phone, the carpet.
- **Over-polite deflection** — a reply that answers a different, safer question
  than the one actually raised.
- **A single ally who almost intervenes** — one onlooker who starts to object, then
  visibly decides it isn't worth it. This one is optional; use it when you want a
  release valve for tension without breaking the obliviousness of the lead.

Keep the onlooker roster small (one to three named reactions) rather than a crowd
reacting in chorus — a crowd reaction reads as broad slapstick, not cringe.

```text
BOSS: — and that's why I always say, diversity of thought is what
matters, not diversity of, you know, whatever.
[Beat.]
DEV (not looking up from her laptop): Mm.
[Beat continues one line longer than comfortable.]
```
The unfilled beat after "Mm" is doing the comedic work — it signals that nobody in
the room is going to be the one to explain what just happened.

## The Escalation Ladder

Structure the scene as a ladder of missteps, each slightly worse than the last,
rather than one big misstep followed by fallout. A workable default shape:

1. **Setup misstep** — establishes the delusion in a low-stakes way.
2. **First correction attempt** — character senses something landed oddly and tries
   to clarify. The clarification contains a second, smaller misstep.
3. **Second correction attempt** — character, now slightly defensive, doubles down
   on the *frame* rather than the specific words, making the underlying delusion
   more explicit.
4. **Overcorrection** — character tries to prove the delusion with a demonstration
   ("Let me show you how comfortable I am with this") that fails harder than the
   original line.
5. **Comeuppance** — see below.

You do not need exactly five beats — three is often enough for a short sketch, five
or more for a longer scene — but resist collapsing straight from beat 1 to
comeuppance. The digging is the joke; the fall is just the punctuation.

## The Recovery-Digging Pattern

When the character tries to recover from a misstep, the attempt should worsen
things by revealing more of the delusion, not by introducing an unrelated new
flaw. Each recovery line should be a direct, logical (to the character) extension
of the belief that caused the original misstep.

```text
MISSTEP: I'm basically an honorary member of every minority in this
building.

RECOVERY (worsens by extending the same belief, not a new one):
Which is why I can say it. It's like reverse racism, except good,
because I'm the one saying it.
```

```text
WEAKER RECOVERY (introduces an unrelated flaw instead of digging):
Also I forgot to submit the expense report, sorry, separate issue.
```
The weaker version breaks the pattern — it is funny in a different, scattered way,
but it abandons the specific delusion instead of deepening it.

## Designing the Comeuppance

The comeuppance should be proportionate to the delusion, not to the offence —
punish the *self-image*, not just the behaviour. If the delusion was "I'm the cool
boss," the comeuppance is being visibly, publicly unhip in front of the person
whose approval the boss most wanted. If the delusion was "I'm great at eulogies,"
the comeuppance is delivering the eulogy and realising, too late, that everyone is
uncomfortable rather than moved.

Two failure directions to watch for:

- **Too mild**: the character shrugs it off with no cost, and the scene reads as
  aimless rather than escalating toward something.
- **Too harsh**: the character is destroyed (fired, publicly humiliated beyond
  recovery) in a way that swaps comedy for cruelty and makes the audience feel bad
  for laughing. Consider leaving the character's job/status intact and instead
  costing them the thing they actually wanted (respect, being seen as cool,
  being liked) — that keeps it a comedy of ego rather than a tragedy.

## Tone Control: Keeping It Funny, Not Just Awkward

"Awkward" alone is not "funny." What keeps it funny:

- The character's logic, however wrong, should be internally consistent — they are
  not stupid, they are self-deceived. Stupidity is a different (weaker) joke.
- There should be a kernel of a real, recognisable social dynamic underneath the
  exaggeration — a real thing bosses/relatives/hosts actually do, pushed further.
- Specificity beats generality. "He said something racist" is not a joke. The
  precise, absurd wording of what he said is the joke.

## Common Failure Modes

- **The character becomes self-aware mid-scene.** Any line where the character
  says or implies "oh no, did I just—" collapses the gap. If the request calls for
  a moment of dawning realisation, put it at the very end, after the comeuppance,
  not during the escalation.
- **Onlookers narrate the joke.** A line like "That's really inappropriate, sir" or
  "Wow, I can't believe he just said that" does the audience's interpretive work
  for them. Cut it; let a reaction do the same job.
- **No ladder — one misstep and straight to fallout.** Reads as an anecdote, not a
  built scene. Add at least one recovery-digging beat.
- **Comeuppance unrelated to the delusion.** A boss whose delusion is "I'm the fun
  boss" being fired for an unrelated HR violation is not a punchline for the
  established joke — it is a different story.

## Worked Example Walkthrough

A short annotated original scene showing the pattern end to end (invented for this
skill, not drawn from any existing script):

```text
[Office kitchen. MANAGER holds a mug that says "WORLD'S MOST
INCLUSIVE BOSS." TWO STAFFERS are making tea.]

MANAGER: You know what I love about this team? Nobody here sees
colour. I mean I do, obviously, I see it, I just don't mind it.
[Beat. STAFFER 1 stirs her tea very slowly.]

MANAGER: Which is rare, by the way. Most people my age are basically
dinosaurs about this stuff. I'm practically one of you.
[STAFFER 2 opens the fridge and stays there a beat too long.]

MANAGER: I'll prove it — ask me anything. Go on. Nothing's off
limits with me, that's the whole point I'm making.
[Silence. Nobody asks anything. MANAGER waits, visibly proud,
mistaking the silence for being too impressed to speak.]

MANAGER: See? People don't even have questions. That's how
comfortable I've made everyone.
[STAFFER 1 finally speaks, flatly.]
STAFFER 1: I have a question. Is the milk still good?
[MANAGER, slightly deflated but recovering instantly, holds up the
mug.]
MANAGER: This mug was a gift, by the way. From the team. Anonymous,
but I have a good idea who.
```

Notes on why this works: the delusion ("I'm inclusive") is specific and stated
early; each beat extends the same delusion rather than introducing a new flaw; the
onlookers never explain the joke, they just withhold the response the manager is
fishing for; the "comeuppance" (nobody has a question, and the one question that
comes is about milk) punishes the specific thing the manager wanted — to be asked
something proving how comfortable everyone is — without destroying him.
