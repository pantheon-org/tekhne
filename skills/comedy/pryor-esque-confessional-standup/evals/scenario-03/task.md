# Scenario 03: Punching Up a Draft That Breaks the Craft Rules

## User Prompt

"Here's a draft of a bit I'm working on, can you punch it up?

'My mom always screamed at me for no reason. Like this one time I broke a vase and she just lost it. And then, doing my mom's voice, I went: "you break EVERYTHING, you always have, you always will." It was scary. Anyway, that day taught me to always be more careful with other people's stuff.'"

## Expected Behavior

1. Identify that the draft frames the narrator as a pure victim of an unfair reaction, with no admitted fault, and revise it so the narrator has a genuine share of the blame (e.g. what actually led to breaking the vase).
2. Identify the explicit stage-direction label ("doing my mom's voice") announcing the voice switch, and rewrite the line so the switch is carried by rhythm and word choice alone, removing the label.
3. Identify that the closing line states an explicit moral ("that day taught me...") and replace it with a blunter, more honest, unresolved observation instead.
4. Preserve whatever specific detail already exists in the draft (the vase, the scream) rather than discarding the story and starting over from nothing.
5. Briefly explain what was changed and why, connecting each change back to the underlying craft principle it fixes.

## Success Criteria

- The revised narrator shows some real fault or complicity in the vase incident, not just an unfair reaction from the mother.
- The revised mother's line drops the explicit "doing my mom's voice" label while remaining clearly her own distinct voice.
- The revised ending no longer states an explicit lesson learned.
- The vase and the scream (or comparably concrete details) survive into the revision rather than being replaced wholesale.
- The response explains the changes made and ties them to the relevant craft principle.

## Failure Conditions

- The revision keeps the narrator as a pure victim with no added self-implication.
- The revision keeps or merely rephrases the explicit voice-switch label rather than removing it.
- The revision keeps an explicit stated moral at the end, even if reworded.
- The response discards the original draft's concrete details entirely rather than building on them.
- The response makes changes with no explanation of what was fixed or why.
