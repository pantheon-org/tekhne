# Extended Metaphor & Narrator Voice

This is a deeper pass on three of the core mechanics from the main skill:
building one metaphor instead of many, keeping the narrator's contempt
balanced between the subject and themselves, and mocking jargon by
translating it. Use this when a rant, column, or monologue is stalling —
usually because the metaphor has drifted, the self-contempt feels bolted
on rather than earned, or the jargon beat reads as a list instead of a
joke.

## Why one metaphor, not several

A string of unrelated jokes about the same subject reads as a list. A
single metaphor, developed and extended, reads as an argument — every new
detail about the subject gets translated into a new detail about the
metaphor, so the piece feels like it's building toward something rather
than just accumulating.

Concretely: pick one *system* (a body, a machine, a bureaucratic process,
a natural disaster, an illness) and map every observation about the
subject onto that system specifically. If the subject is a reality show
elimination format, and the chosen system is "slaughterhouse," then:

- Contestants aren't "eliminated," they're "processed."
- The judges' panel isn't "a panel," it's "the inspection line."
- A contestant's tearful exit interview isn't "an interview," it's "the
  bit where the camera lingers on the one that didn't make weight."

```text
Every week the show runs another one through the line. The judges don't
comment on performances, they grade carcasses — this one's marbled
nicely, that one's mostly gristle — and the studio audience, who paid to
be here, cheers the grading like it's sport instead of an inspection.
```

Note that nothing in that paragraph introduces a second unrelated image.
The temptation, once you've got a laugh from "the inspection line," is to
reach for a completely different joke about the judges next. Resist it —
extend the same system instead. That's the difference between an extended
metaphor and a joke list wearing one metaphor as a hat.

### Choosing a metaphor that scales

Not every image can carry a whole piece. A metaphor scales well if it has
internal stages or escalating severity built in — a disease that
progresses, a machine that breaks down in stages, a bureaucracy with
layers to move through. A metaphor that's just one static image (a single
photograph, a single object with no internal process) runs out of new
angles after a sentence or two.

Good scaling candidates: an illness with named stages, a factory
production line, a court proceeding, a slow-moving natural disaster, a
species going through extinction. Consider whichever fits the subject's
own shape — a subscription service that gets worse in stages suits a
disease metaphor; a one-time viral news story suits something faster,
like a car crash or a structure fire.

## Balancing contempt: subject vs. self

The narrator needs to look down on the subject, but a narrator who only
ever punches outward reads as smug rather than funny — and smugness kills
the tone this skill is going for. The fix is to give the narrator's own
habits, hypocrisies, or complicity a share of the same contempt,
regularly, not just once as a token gesture near the top.

A useful ratio to aim for, loosely: for every two or three jabs at the
subject, land at least one jab at the narrator or the audience. It
doesn't need to be mechanical — you can weight it however suits the piece
— but a piece that never turns the metaphor on the narrator tends to feel
one-note.

```text
I have opinions about people who can't put their phones down during a
film. I also checked mine four times during the last one I watched, twice
to see if anyone had liked something I posted about how nobody should be
on their phones during films.
```

The self-directed jab doesn't need its own separate metaphor system — it
can borrow the same one. If the reality-show piece is running the
slaughterhouse metaphor, the narrator's own participation (watching,
enjoying, coming back next week) can get graded on the same line:

```text
I watch it every week. I have, by any reasonable definition, been
grading the carcasses right alongside them, from the sofa, with a glass
of wine, telling myself that noticing the process makes me exempt from
it. It doesn't.
```

### When to dial it up or down

This is a technique to use, not a mandatory quota on every paragraph. A
short, punchy piece might land the self-contempt once, hard, near the
end, and spend the rest of its length on the subject. A longer, more
personal piece can weave it through continuously. Either is fine —
consider what the specific piece needs rather than forcing an even split.

## Jargon mockery: quote, then translate

This beat has a simple two-part shape: quote the institutional or
corporate phrasing (invented, not lifted from a real source unless the
user has supplied it themselves), then translate it plainly into what it
actually means. The comedy is in the gap between the two — the plainer
and flatter the translation, the funnier the gap reads.

```text
The update notes describe the change as "streamlining the user journey
around core engagement touchpoints." What actually happened is that they
removed the button that let you turn notifications off, and renamed the
setting that used to do that "Focus Mode," which now costs £2.99 a month.
```

A few things make this land:

- Keep the quoted phrase itself plausible — real corporate language is
  already slightly absurd; you don't need to exaggerate it, just quote it
  straight-faced.
- Make the translation shorter and blunter than the quote. The length
  contrast between the euphemism and the plain statement is doing the
  comedic work, similar to the hyperbole-then-bathos beat elsewhere in
  this skill.
- You can optionally follow the translation with one more beat of
  contempt (for the company, or for the narrator's own willingness to
  keep paying), but it isn't required — sometimes the flat translation is
  the whole joke and anything after it dilutes it.

## Sentence rhythm: build, then puncture

The elaborate-sentence-then-blunt-clause pattern works because it mimics
the shape of the joke itself: tension building, then released fast. In
practice this usually means:

1. One or two long sentences that extend the metaphor with more clauses,
   more detail, more escalating imagery.
2. A short sentence — often under ten words — that either states the
   mundane reality plainly, or delivers the self-contempt jab, or both.

```text
The metaphor can run for as long as the momentum holds — clause stacked on
clause, each one adding another layer of grotesque detail to the same
image, the sentence practically refusing to end because ending it would
mean admitting how small the actual subject is. Then it's just a kettle.
```

You don't need to hit this pattern every paragraph. Consider using it at
the peaks of the piece — the end of a metaphor-building passage, or right
before a scene or section break — rather than in every single sentence,
which would flatten the rhythm rather than create it.
