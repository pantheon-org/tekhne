# Dystopian Escalation Structure

This is a deeper pass on the narrative form of this skill: taking a
plausible near-future technology or social mechanic and extending it, one
logical consequence at a time, into a nightmarish but internally
consistent outcome. Use this when the short-story form is stalling —
usually because the escalation has stalled flat, jumped illogically, or
the ending keeps drifting toward something hopeful.

## The shape of the chain

Think of the escalation as a chain of small, individually reasonable
decisions, where each link is justified by the one before it, and only
the *sum* of the chain is horrifying. No single step should feel like a
leap — if a reader could plausibly imagine a real company or institution
making that exact decision for that exact reason, the step is doing its
job.

A reliable pattern for building the chain:

1. **The premise.** State the starting technology or mechanic in one
   sentence, as it might actually be pitched today. Keep it small and
   specific — "a fridge that suggests recipes," not "AI takes over the
   home."
2. **The first small step.** The most obviously convenient extension of
   the premise — the one a product team would ship first because it
   tests well.
3. **The step that removes a choice.** Something that was previously
   optional becomes default, or something that was free starts requiring
   effort or money to avoid.
4. **The step that monetises the removal.** The company finds a way to
   charge for the friction it just created, usually framed as a premium
   feature or a "convenience fee."
5. **The step that turns data collection into consequence.** Information
   gathered along the way starts feeding a decision that affects the
   person in a domain they didn't expect (insurance, employment,
   healthcare, social standing).
6. **The step where institutions normalise it.** Other systems start
   treating the mechanic as ordinary — a school, an employer, a
   government service references it as though it were unremarkable.
7. **The final, worse return to the premise.** The story closes by
   coming back to the original small mechanic, now grown into something
   that makes the original pitch look naive.

Not every piece needs all seven beats — a short piece might compress
several into one paragraph, and a longer one might slow down and dwell on
a single step. Consider the length you're aiming for and compress or
expand accordingly; the point is the *order* and the *logic*, not hitting
a fixed beat count.

## A worked example

Premise: a fitness-tracking app that reminds you to stand up.

```text
Step 1 (premise): The watch buzzes when you've been sitting too long.
Step 2 (convenience): The watch starts suggesting a short walk based on
  your calendar, so you don't have to think about when to take it.
Step 3 (removes a choice): Turning off the reminders requires a written
  reason, logged and reviewed monthly by "a wellness partner," because
  the company's insurer asked for engagement data as a condition of
  keeping premiums flat.
Step 4 (monetises the removal): Opting out entirely costs a monthly fee,
  because the insurer's discount is now contingent on participation, and
  the company passes the difference on directly.
Step 5 (data becomes consequence): A hiring platform starts asking
  candidates to "optionally" share twelve months of activity data as
  part of a wellness-culture-fit score, and declining is logged as a data
  point too.
Step 6 (normalised): A school district adopts the same watch for staff,
  citing the insurer discount, and describes non-participation in the
  staff handbook as "a flag for pastoral follow-up."
Step 7 (return, worse): The watch still buzzes when you've sat too long.
  Nobody remembers agreeing to any of the rest of it. Standing up is now
  the only part of the original idea nobody's found a way to charge for
  yet, and that's presented, without irony, as the good news.
```

Each step names a plausible, specific mechanism (an insurer's condition,
a fee, a scoring system, a handbook policy) rather than an abstract
worsening ("things got more surveilled"). Specificity is what keeps each
step feeling earned rather than assumed.

## Guarding against the illogical jump

The most common failure mode is a step that requires a leap the reader
hasn't been given a reason for — often because it's more dramatic than
the step that would actually follow. Before finalising an escalation
chain, check each step against the one before it by asking: "would a
real institution plausibly do this, for the reason given, based on what
was just established?" If the answer requires inventing a new
capability, motive, or actor that hasn't been set up, the step needs
either more groundwork earlier in the chain, or a smaller, more earned
substitute.

```text
❌ BAD — "The watch tracked steps. Then, without explanation, it began
   controlling the wearer's front door lock."

✅ GOOD — "The watch tracked steps. The building's insurer offered a
   premium discount for buildings that verified staff wellness compliance
   at entry, so the front door started checking the watch's sync status
   before letting people in."
```

The second version adds a motive (an insurer discount), a mechanism
(entry verification tied to sync status), and a reason the building would
adopt it — three small, ordinary decisions rather than one large,
unexplained one.

## Guarding against the hopeful ending

The escalation chain builds pressure specifically so that the ending can
release it flatly rather than resolve it. A last-minute reversal,
realisation, or act of resistance undoes that pressure and changes the
piece's genre from bleak satire to a cautionary tale with a moral — which
may be a perfectly good thing to write, but it isn't what this skill
teaches.

Practical check: read the final paragraph in isolation. If it contains a
character learning something, taking an action that fixes the situation,
or experiencing relief, that's very likely too hopeful for this form.
Consider instead ending on: the mechanic continuing unchanged, a detail
that shows the situation has quietly gotten worse since the last section,
or the narrator explicitly noting that nothing will be done about it.

```text
❌ BAD ending — "She finally uninstalled the app, breathed the fresh air
   of not being tracked, and resolved to live more simply from now on."

✅ GOOD ending — "She uninstalled the app. The building's front door
   stopped opening for her the following Monday, and the notice taped to
   it, when she finally read it, thanked her for her flexibility during
   the transition."
```
