# Deadpan Delivery and Abrupt Endings

This reference covers two problems that surface after a sketch's premise and escalation are
already working: keeping every character's delivery deadpan rather than "trying to be funny," and
choosing an ending that lands as abrupt or anticlimactic rather than as a tidy resolution.

## What deadpan actually requires

Deadpan is not "the character doesn't smile." It's that the character's *stakes are real to
them*. A clerk enforcing an absurd form requirement is not performing absurdity: as far as they
are concerned, they are doing an ordinary, mildly tedious job, and the form requirement is simply
correct. The comedy is entirely the audience's; the character never accesses it.

This has a concrete writing consequence: every line a deadpan character speaks should be
something they would say in a version of the scene where the premise wasn't absurd at all. Test a
line by asking "would a genuinely unremarkable person in a genuinely unremarkable version of this
job say this, in this tone?" If the answer is yes, it's deadpan. If the line only makes sense as a
wink at the audience, it's broken.

### Spotting a broken line before it ships

```text
❌ CLERK: I know this sounds completely mad, but you'll need form 7B.
```

The clause "I know this sounds completely mad" is the character acknowledging the joke. Compare:

```text
✅ CLERK: You'll need form 7B.
APPLICANT: This is completely mad.
CLERK: It's mad from where you're standing. From here it's Tuesday.
```

The second version keeps the audience's reaction (via the applicant) without handing it to the
clerk. The clerk's line is *about* the absurdity but stays entirely inside the scene's own logic:
"mad from where you're standing" is a genuine, deadpan defence of the process, not a wink.

## Distributing deadpan across multiple characters

A common weak spot: one character (the "straight man") reacts normally while another delivers all
the absurdity, and the straight man's incredulity becomes a running commentary that starts to feel
like the show explaining itself. Two fixes:

1. **Give the straight man their own escalating logic too.** Instead of pure disbelief, let their
   objections accumulate their own rules (see the Escalation reference for how objections can
   generate further complications rather than just restating "this is absurd").
2. **Consider making everyone equally deadpan.** A scene where every character, including
   newcomers, immediately accepts the absurd premise as routine is often funnier than one where a
   single voice of reason keeps the audience company, because it removes the audience's
   surrogate entirely and commits fully to the internal logic.

Neither approach is mandatory; which one fits depends on whether the sketch benefits from an
audience surrogate (useful in longer, more layered escalations) or from total commitment (useful
in shorter, tighter bits).

## Choosing an ending

Three ending shapes recur. None is intrinsically better; the choice depends on where the
escalation has taken the scene.

### Dry anticlimax

The escalation simply stops on something smaller or more mundane than the momentum suggested,
without comment.

```text
INSPECTOR: This could go all the way to a tribunal.
OWNER: A tribunal? Over a doormat?
INSPECTOR: [checking watch] Actually, my shift ended four minutes ago. Same time Thursday?
```

Best fit: escalations built on bureaucratic procedure, where the process itself running out of
steam (rather than resolving) is the funniest possible outcome.

### Sudden non sequitur

The scene is cut off by something entirely unrelated to the established logic, delivered with the
same deadpan commitment as everything before it.

```text
CLERK: ...and so your appeal is denied pending review of the reviewer's own appeal.
APPLICANT: Then what do I...
[A second clerk enters, carrying a tuba.]
SECOND CLERK: Is this still the queue for stamps?
```

Best fit: escalations that have become tightly self-referential (rules about rules): a non
sequitur breaks the loop rather than trying to top it.

### Reversal

The rule or authority driving the escalation is suddenly, deadpan-ly revealed to apply to itself,
or to have been pointless from a new angle, without anyone remarking on the irony.

```text
OFFICIAL: I'm afraid this form itself is out of date.
APPLICANT: Since when?
OFFICIAL: Since we introduced a new form for reporting out-of-date forms. Which is also out of
          date, but nobody's filed the form for that yet. Perhaps you'd like to be first.
```

Best fit: escalations built on pedantry or semantic argument, where the joke of the ending is that
the definitional trap the characters have been fighting through was already big enough to catch
its own rule.

## Avoiding the tidy-resolution trap

The failure mode to guard against is an ending that explains, apologises for, or morally resolves
the absurdity, because it retroactively frames the escalation as a problem needing a solution,
rather than a system worth inhabiting to the end. A quick check: if the last line could be followed
by "...and that taught them a valuable lesson," rewrite the ending. None of the three shapes above
should ever require that sentence to make sense.
