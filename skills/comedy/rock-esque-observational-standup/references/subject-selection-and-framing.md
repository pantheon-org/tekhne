# Subject Selection and Framing

A deep dive on choosing and framing the subject of a bit so the claim reads as sharp, earned observation rather than a mean-spirited or lazy generalisation. Use this file when picking or narrowing a subject, or when a drafted claim feels like it's punching down rather than punching at something worth hitting.

## What makes a subject work for this style

The mechanics in the main skill file (contrastive claim, escalating examples, refrain, aphoristic close) will technically run on almost any subject. What separates a sharp bit from a lazy one is whether the *underlying claim* is actually observed, and whether it targets a behaviour or a hypocrisy rather than a group of people for existing.

Subjects that work well tend to share three properties:

1. **The claim is about behaviour, not identity.** "There's a difference between generous and generous-in-front-of-people" targets a behaviour anyone can recognise in themselves. A claim that instead just restates a stereotype about a group of people, with no behavioural mechanism underneath it, isn't an observation — it's just the stereotype with a rhythm applied to it.
2. **The audience recognises it in themselves, not only in others.** The strongest version of this style implicates the audience along with (or instead of) some outside target. If every example points outward at "them" and never lands on "us," the bit tends to read as contempt rather than observation, and it also tends to be structurally weaker, because self-implication is what makes the direct-address ("you know what I mean") technique from the main file actually land.
3. **There's a real hypocrisy or contradiction to expose.** The strongest claims in this tradition — about class, respectability, media coverage, relationship dynamics — work because they surface a gap between what people say and what they actually do. If the claim doesn't have that gap, it's an observation about a difference in taste or preference, which is a weaker foundation for this particular structure (though still usable for a lighter bit).

## Suggested subjects and how to frame them

These are starting points, not an exhaustive or exclusive list — a user's own subject, framed against the three properties above, will usually work just as well.

- **Respectability politics.** Frame as a gap between the standard someone holds *others* to and the standard they apply to *themselves* or people they like. The contrast pair is usually "the version of this that gets praised" vs. "the identical version of this that gets criticised," differing only in who's doing it.
- **Class and money.** Frame as a gap between what money actually buys and what people claim it buys (freedom, taste, work ethic). The strongest version of this subject usually contrasts two people at *different* wealth levels doing the *same* thing, where the label applied to the behaviour changes depending on who's doing it.
- **Media hypocrisy.** Frame as a gap between the story a piece of coverage claims to be telling and the story its own choices (what it leads with, what it leaves out, what it frames as shocking) actually tell. The examples work best when they're about coverage patterns rather than a single named outlet or person, unless the user has specifically asked for the latter.
- **Relationship dynamics.** Frame as a gap between what a phrase is supposed to communicate ("I'm fine," "no rush," "we should hang out sometime") and what everyone in the room already knows it actually communicates. This subject leans hardest on the audience recognising themselves, which is why it's a reliable warm-up subject.
- **Gender dynamics.** Frame as a gap in an expectation applied unevenly, illustrated through matched examples — the same action, described or received differently depending on who does it. Matched pairs (not lopsided ones) keep this subject reading as observation rather than a one-sided complaint.
- **Race relations.** This is the highest-risk subject on this list and the one most likely to misfire into "punching down" if the claim targets a group's identity rather than a specific, named social dynamic (e.g. a double standard in how the same behaviour is received). If the user requests this subject, hold the claim to all three properties above particularly strictly, and prefer a claim about how *institutions or observers* behave over a claim about how *a racial group* behaves.

## A quick check before drafting examples

Before writing the escalating examples for a drafted claim, run it through this check:

- **Swap test:** could the claim be restated about a different group entirely and still make the same point? If yes, the claim is really about a behaviour (good — that's portable and defensible). If the claim falls apart the moment you swap the group, it likely wasn't about a behaviour to begin with.
- **Mirror test:** does at least one example implicate the audience, the narrator, or a sympathetic figure, rather than only an outside target? If every example points only outward, consider adding one that points inward before moving on to escalation.
- **Specificity test:** can the claim be traced to an actual observed behaviour with a concrete example, or does it only work as a generalisation? If you can't produce one genuinely concrete, plausible scene illustrating it, the claim may need narrowing before it's ready for the escalation structure.

If a drafted claim fails the swap test or the mirror test, it usually doesn't need to be discarded — narrowing it (from "a whole group does X" to "here's a specific behaviour, and here's who actually does it") is often enough to fix it while keeping the sharpness the user was going for.
