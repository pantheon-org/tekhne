# High-Energy Observational Stand-Up

Build comedy around a bold, sweeping claim about society, relationships, race, or class, argued with rhythmic conviction rather than a string of unrelated one-liners.

## Mindset

The laugh comes from *recognition*, not information: the bit works because the audience already half-believed the claim and was too polite to say it, so grounding it in small concrete detail turns a lecture into a confession everyone shares.

Expected: the room laughs at recognising something it already half-knew, not at being handed new information — every technique below serves that one mechanism.

## Prerequisites

Before drafting, check whether the user has named a subject (race, gender, class, money, respectability, media hypocrisy, or their own topic) or is happy for you to pick one. Read back the claim in one sentence before drafting examples, so the user can redirect early if it's not the angle they wanted. This skill always assumes original wording — it requires the request to be for new material, not a transcription of an existing special.

## When to Use

- The user asks for a stand-up bit, routine, or monologue with a punchline.
- The user wants something structured as a bold claim proven through escalating examples ("there's a difference between X and Y").
- The user wants rhythmic, rising-energy delivery rather than a flat list of one-liners.
- The user references a Chris-Rock-style observational voice but wants **original** material, not a transcription of an existing special.

## When NOT to Use

- The user wants a sketch with multiple characters acting out a scene — use a sketch-writing approach instead.
- The user wants confessional or narrative storytelling comedy (a single extended anecdote, not an escalating argument).
- The user wants a flat list of unrelated one-liners or puns with no contrastive claim or escalation.
- The user asks you to transcribe, paraphrase closely, or "do" an existing comedian's actual routine — UNLESS they've separately confirmed they only want the structural technique, decline that specific request and offer original material built on the same structure instead.

## Workflow

1. **Set the claim.** Pick one bold, sweeping claim about the given subject and frame it as an obvious truth the audience has been too polite to say out loud.
2. **Frame the contrast.** Cast the claim as "there's a difference between X and Y," where X and Y are two recognisable types of person or behaviour.
3. **Draft 3-5 escalating examples.** Each proves the contrast with a concrete, everyday scenario, ordered so each one is more pointed than the last.
4. **Choose a refrain.** An optional but effective device: pick a short contrastive phrase from step 2 or 3 that can recur, sharpened slightly each time by what the examples just established.
5. **Add one imagined objector.** Voice a brief counter-argument the audience might raise, then knock it down in a line or two before continuing the escalation.
6. **Shape the rhythm.** Write in short, punchy clauses that rise in intensity; a hard stop or tonal drop right before the close is one effective option for emphasis.
7. **Write the closer.** One tight, quotable, aphoristic line that reframes the whole bit — never hedge it (see `## Anti-Patterns`).
8. **Check the draft.** Run through the checklist in `## Troubleshooting` below, then verify the bit is ready to deliver.

## Core Mechanics

These are techniques to draw on, not a fixed checklist — a strong bit typically uses most of them, but you can weight them differently depending on subject and tone.

- Open with a blunt, generalised claim stated as an obvious truth the audience has been too polite to say out loud.
- Build the bit as a contrast: "there's a difference between X and Y," proven through a sequence of increasingly pointed, escalating examples.
- Build rhythmically — short, punchy clauses that rise in intensity and volume. A drop in tone right before the punchline is one option for emphasis; a straight rising build works too.
- A refrain across the bit is optional for very short material, but earns its keep in anything over a minute: each recurrence should be sharpened by what came before it.
- Direct address and rhetorical questions ("you know what I mean") can make the claim feel like shared, obvious knowledge — use them where the bit benefits from a conspiratorial tone.
- Keep the objector brief: voice a typical counter-argument in a line or two, then knock it down before continuing to build. AVOID stacking more than one objector into a single bit — it drags.
- PREFER concrete, everyday comparisons over abstract argument — ground big social claims in small, specific, relatable scenarios.
- Keep mock outrage escalating the bit as examples pile up, while staying in control of the joke; a cooler, deadpan build is an equally valid alternative register.
- Land on a tight, quotable, aphoristic line that reframes the whole bit in a single sentence.
- Test the opening line by itself before building the rest of the bit — if it doesn't land as an obvious truth on its own, the escalation won't save it.
- Useful subjects include race relations, gender dynamics, class and money, respectability politics, and media hypocrisy — but the mechanics transfer to other subjects the user proposes.

## Anti-Patterns

**NEVER** reproduce a real performer's actual routines, bits, or specific phrasing verbatim or near-verbatim.

**WHY:** it is copyrighted material, and reproducing it defeats the purpose of a *generative* skill — the user gets a copy of someone else's work, not new material of their own.

**BAD:**
```text
There's rich, and there's wealthy. Shaq is rich. The man who signs
Shaq's checks is wealthy.
```

**GOOD:**
```text
There's saving, and there's being "good with money." Saving is a
Tupperware full of coins on top of the fridge. Being good with money
is asking the barista for a receipt you will never look at again.
```

**SYMPTOM:** the output reads like a transcript of an existing special — matching rhythm, matching examples, sometimes matching words — instead of something built fresh from the technique.

**CONSEQUENCE:** the output is unusable (infringing, and dishonest about its origin) and has to be discarded and rewritten from scratch anyway.

**NEVER** let the examples stay abstract.

**WHY:** the bit's power comes from grounding a big social claim in small, concrete, everyday scenarios; abstract argument reads as a lecture, not a joke.

**BAD:**
```text
There's a difference between being polite and being honest. Society
often confuses the two, which causes a lot of unspoken tension in
relationships.
```

**GOOD:**
```text
There's polite, and there's honest. Polite is telling your friend
the casserole was "interesting." Honest is the fork staying on the
plate for the rest of the night.
```

**SYMPTOM:** every "example" is really just a restatement of the claim in slightly different words, with no scene, no image, nothing an audience could picture.

**CONSEQUENCE:** the bit loses its laugh-generating structure entirely and becomes generic op-ed prose with line breaks.

**NEVER** soften the closing line with a qualifier.

**WHY:** the aphoristic punchline needs to land flat and stand alone; hedging it after the fact drains the rhythm the whole bit built toward.

**BAD:**
```text
...and that's the difference. At least, that's how it feels to me,
your mileage may vary, no offense to anyone.
```

**GOOD:**
```text
...and that's the difference. One of them means it. The other one
just wants credit for saying it.
```

**SYMPTOM:** the last line runs on past its natural stopping point, adding a disclaimer, a laugh-deflector, or a "just kidding" walk-back.

**CONSEQUENCE:** the final beat — the one line the audience was meant to walk out repeating — gets buried under a disclaimer, and the bit ends on a shrug instead of a hit.

## Examples

Five short, freshly invented snippets, each demonstrating one technique in isolation.

**1. Blunt opening claim stated as obvious truth:**

```text
Everybody in this room has said "I'm fine" and meant "come back in
five minutes when I've figured out how mad I actually am."
```

*What it demonstrates:* opens with a claim the audience recognises instantly, framed as something they already knew but hadn't said aloud — that recognition is where the first laugh comes from, before a single example arrives.

**2. Contrastive frame with escalating examples:**

```text
There's a difference between being early and being anxious. Early is
showing up ten minutes ahead. Anxious is showing up ten minutes ahead,
then re-reading the invite to check it's not next Tuesday. Anxious is
googling the restaurant's cancellation policy for a table you haven't
booked yet.
```

*What it demonstrates:* each example is a small escalation on the last, moving from mildly relatable to specifically, uncomfortably precise — that's the rising energy of the bit doing its job.

**3. Sharpened refrain returning across a bit:**

```text
There's helping, and there's "helping." [...examples...] See, that's
"helping" again — this time with a receipt so everyone knows about it.
```

*What it demonstrates:* the second appearance of the refrain reuses the exact contrastive phrase but adds a new twist ("with a receipt") built from what the intervening examples established, so the callback feels earned rather than repeated.

**4. Imagined objector voiced then defeated:**

```text
Now somebody's gonna say, "well, everybody's just doing their best."
Sure. Some people's best takes four minutes. Some people's best takes
four phone calls and a group chat vote.
```

*What it demonstrates:* the objection is stated fairly, in its own voice, so knocking it down in the next line feels like a fair fight the bit won, not a strawman — that's what keeps the escalation going instead of stalling it.

**5. Unhedged aphoristic closer:**

```text
So the next time somebody tells you they're "just being honest" —
check whether you asked.
```

*What it demonstrates:* one sentence, no qualifier, that reframes every example that came before it — landing flat like this is what makes it quotable on the way out of the room.

## Troubleshooting

If the closing line's edge is missing, check for a hidden qualifier and cut it — see the third anti-pattern above.
If the examples still read flat after a rewrite, check whether they're actually escalating or just restating the same point three times.
If a subject feels like it's punching down rather than observing, see [Subject Selection and Framing](references/subject-selection-and-framing.md) and narrow the claim to a behaviour.

Run through this short checklist, then verify the bit is ready to deliver:

- Check that every example is a concrete scene, not an abstract statement.
- Verify the refrain, if used, sharpens with each return rather than repeating unchanged.
- Ensure the closing line carries no hedge, qualifier, or disclaimer.
- Confirm the escalation actually rises: read the examples in order and check the energy climbs.
