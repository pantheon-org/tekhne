# Institutional Targets

A deep dive on adapting the deadpan two-person interview form to institutional targets other than a government minister — corporate spokespeople, NGOs, regulators, and frontline agency staff each need a slightly different register to stay believable while keeping the same mechanics.

## Why the target's register matters

The core mechanics (terse questions, literal-minded confidence, escalating contradiction, flat close) stay identical across targets. What changes is the *vocabulary of confidence* — the specific kind of institutional language that sounds ordinary to that interviewee but reads as absurd once questioned. Government-minister satire leans on policy and mandate language; a corporate spokesperson leans on a different vocabulary entirely, and using the wrong register is the fastest way to make a sketch feel generic.

## Government or public-office register

- **Vocabulary of confidence**: "the government remains committed to," "a full and independent review," "on track," "in due course," "a temporary measure."
- **What the interviewer should press on**: timelines, who is accountable, and the gap between an announcement and the mechanism that would actually deliver it.
- **Where the absurdity usually lives**: in the distance between a promise and any concrete date, number, or named person responsible for it.

```text
Interviewer: When will the review conclude?
Minister: In due course.
Interviewer: Is there a date attached to "in due course"?
Minister: Due course doesn't really work with dates.
```

## Corporate spokesperson register

- **Vocabulary of confidence**: "we're always listening to our customers," "this is part of a broader transformation," "an enhanced experience," "streamlining," "unprecedented demand."
- **What the interviewer should press on**: what a piece of marketing language actually means operationally — a "streamlined" process usually means something was removed, not improved.
- **Where the absurdity usually lives**: in translating soft corporate language into the concrete, worse outcome it's covering for.

```text
Interviewer: You're calling the branch closures a "community banking transformation."
Spokesperson: That's right. We're transforming where the community can bank.
Interviewer: To nowhere nearby.
Spokesperson: To wherever is most efficient for us to offer that.
```

Consider giving the corporate interviewee slightly warmer, more customer-service-inflected phrasing than a government minister would use — "we hear you," "we value that feedback" — since the mismatch between warmth and the actual bad news is itself part of what makes this register land.

## NGO or advocacy-body register

- **Vocabulary of confidence**: "raising awareness," "convening stakeholders," "a call to action," "we've made real progress."
- **What the interviewer should press on**: the gap between activity (meetings, statements, campaigns) and any measurable outcome.
- **Where the absurdity usually lives**: an NGO spokesperson defending process as if it were itself the result.

```text
Interviewer: So after three years, what's actually changed?
Officer: We've convened four stakeholder roundtables.
Interviewer: And the issue itself?
Officer: Is now very well understood by everyone at those roundtables.
```

## Frontline agency or call-centre register

- **Vocabulary of confidence**: "that's not something I'm able to action from this line," "you'd need to submit that in writing," "I can see that on the system but I can't change it."
- **What the interviewer should press on**: the frontline worker isn't the one with power, so the interviewer's questions should aim past them at the system they're defending, without making the interviewee cruel or unsympathetic — the comedy is bureaucratic distance, not personal callousness.
- **Where the absurdity usually lives**: a reasonable person trapped defending a process they didn't design and can't fix, answered with total procedural calm rather than frustration.

```text
Interviewer: So no one can approve this.
Agent: Not from this department, no.
Interviewer: Which department can?
Agent: I'm not able to see that information from here.
```

## Regulator or oversight-body register

- **Vocabulary of confidence**: "within our statutory remit," "we take a proportionate approach," "we've issued guidance," "this is being monitored closely."
- **What the interviewer should press on**: the difference between having a power and having used it — a regulator that "monitors closely" for years without acting is a rich target.
- **Where the absurdity usually lives**: an oversight body whose entire function has quietly become observation rather than intervention.

```text
Interviewer: How many enforcement actions has the regulator taken this year?
Spokesperson: None. We prefer engagement over enforcement.
Interviewer: And last year?
Spokesperson: Also engagement. It's been a very engaged few years.
```

## Academic or research-institute register

- **Vocabulary of confidence**: "further research is needed," "a robust methodology," "peer-reviewed," "preliminary findings suggest."
- **What the interviewer should press on**: how long "further research" has been the answer, and what would actually count as enough evidence to act.
- **Where the absurdity usually lives**: certainty about the process of finding out, paired with total non-commitment about ever actually finding out.

```text
Interviewer: This is the third preliminary study on the same question.
Researcher: Fourth, if you count the pilot.
Interviewer: When does a preliminary study stop being preliminary?
Researcher: That would need its own study.
```

## Matching register to the interviewer, not just the interviewee

The examples above focus on the interviewee's vocabulary, but the interviewer's phrasing should shift slightly to match the world the sketch is set in too — a business-press interviewer presses on numbers and shareholder impact, a general news interviewer presses on public impact and timelines, and a trade-press interviewer (education press, health press) presses on sector-specific consequences. It's fine to keep the interviewer mostly neutral and let the register live mainly in the interviewee's answers if a more specific interviewer voice isn't needed for the premise — matching both voices to a register is a refinement, not a requirement for the mechanics to work.

## Mixing registers within one sketch

Occasionally a premise genuinely involves two institutions passing responsibility back and forth — a regulator and the company it regulates, or a government department and the agency that implements its policy. When that's the case, consider a three-line pattern where the interviewer's question about who's responsible gets bounced between registers within a single answer, rather than introducing a third speaking character: the interviewee can quote or paraphrase what "the other side" would say, keeping the sketch at two voices while still capturing the back-and-forth of institutional buck-passing.

```text
Interviewer: So whose decision was it to close the assessment centre?
Spokesperson: That sits with the regional authority.
Interviewer: And the regional authority told us it sits with you.
Spokesperson: That's also correct.
```

## Choosing a register when the user's request is ambiguous

If the user names a general subject ("write a sketch about a housing announcement") without specifying whether the interviewee is a minister, a developer's spokesperson, or a housing-charity officer, picking the government-minister register is a reasonable default — it's the most familiar frame for this style — but note the choice and offer to switch registers if a different one fits the user's actual target better.

## Quick reference: register selection by subject

| If the premise is about... | Consider this register | Because... |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A law, funding decision, or public statement | Government/public-office | Most familiar frame; policy language is widely recognisable |
| A product, service, price, or branding change | Corporate spokesperson | Marketing language provides the clearest confidence vocabulary |
| A campaign, charity programme, or advocacy claim | NGO/advocacy-body | Activity-as-outcome vocabulary is distinctive to this space |
| A queue, application, or individual case decision | Frontline agency | Puts the absurdity in the system, not the individual worker |
| An inspection, licence, or compliance failure | Regulator/oversight-body | Highlights the gap between power and its use |
| A study, statistic, or evidence claim | Academic/research-institute | Certainty-about-process vocabulary fits naturally |

This table is a starting point for a fast decision, not an exhaustive mapping — a premise can straddle two rows, and picking whichever register gives the sharper vocabulary of confidence for that specific joke is more important than matching the table exactly.
