# Escalation Pacing

A deep dive on how many question-answer exchanges a deadpan two-person satire sketch typically needs, and how to judge whether a draft is escalating too fast, too slowly, or not at all.

## Why pacing matters here

The core mechanic of this form is a *single* premise unfolding one logical step at a time. Unlike a monologue, where a comedian can jump between unrelated topics, the two-hander interview has one throughline: a policy or claim gets examined until it collapses under its own logic. Pacing failures usually show up as one of two problems:

- **Too fast**: the interviewer jumps straight to the most absurd implication in question two or three, so there's nowhere left to escalate. The sketch peaks early and then just repeats the same beat.
- **Too slow**: the interviewer keeps asking clarifying questions that don't add new information, so exchanges six through ten feel like padding rather than escalation.

## A rough shape to aim for

Most effective sketches in this style fall into a five-to-nine-exchange shape:

1. **Exchange 1-2 (setup)**: a neutral, almost boring opening question establishes the premise as if it were ordinary. No absurdity is visible yet.
2. **Exchange 3-4 (first turn)**: the interviewer asks a question that surfaces the first hint that the premise doesn't hold together. The interviewee answers with confidence rather than concern.
3. **Exchange 5-7 (escalation body)**: each exchange adds exactly one new fact or consequence that makes the premise harder to defend. This is where repetition of a key term usually does the most work — each repetition should carry slightly more weight than the last.
4. **Exchange 8-9 (close)**: the interviewer's final question invites a summary or concession. The interviewee's answer is the flattest line in the piece and doubles as the punchline.

This is a shape, not a rule — a tight four-exchange sketch can work if the premise is sharp enough that it doesn't need much room to build, and a longer sketch (ten to twelve exchanges) can work if the subject has several independent absurd angles worth visiting in sequence. Use judgement about the specific premise rather than forcing every draft to hit five to nine exchanges exactly.

## Diagnosing an under-escalating draft

If a draft feels flat, check whether each exchange after the setup is doing one of these three things. If an exchange does none of them, it's likely padding:

- Adding a new fact that makes the premise harder to defend.
- Repeating or reframing a key term so it carries more weight than before.
- Setting up the information the closing line will need to land.

A common fix is to delete a middle exchange that only restates the previous one without adding anything, and let the next exchange absorb its function.

## Diagnosing an over-escalating draft

If a draft feels like it "used up" its best material too early, look at exchange two or three. If the interviewee is already contradicting themselves in an obvious way that early, there's no room left to build. Consider moving the sharpest line to the end and replacing the early version with a milder, more plausible-sounding version of the same claim — save the fullest absurdity for the close.

## Repetition as a pacing tool, not a requirement

Repeating a key phrase (a term like "temporary," "independent," "voluntary") across three or four exchanges, each time with slightly more strain on the word's meaning, is one of the most reliable ways to make pacing feel deliberate rather than random. But it's an option, not a mandatory structure — a sketch built entirely on escalating *facts* rather than a repeated word can work just as well, particularly for subjects (numbers, deadlines, statistics) where a repeated word would feel forced. Choose whichever the specific premise supports; don't force a repeated phrase into a sketch where the escalation is naturally numeric or factual.

## Quick self-check before finishing a draft

- Does exchange one give away the ending? If so, move information later.
- Does any middle exchange just restate the previous one? If so, cut or merge it.
- Does the final line add a new fact, or does it calmly restate what's already been established? The latter is usually stronger — new information at the very end can feel like a twist rather than a landing.

## Worked example: tracing the shape through a full sketch

Below is a nine-exchange sketch about a fictional "flexible retirement age" policy, annotated with which pacing beat each exchange is doing. This is original material written to illustrate pacing, not a real transcript.

```text
1 (setup)
Interviewer: You're introducing a flexible retirement age.
Official: That's right. Flexible.

2 (setup)
Interviewer: Flexible meaning people can retire earlier if they choose?
Official: Flexible meaning the age can move. Not necessarily earlier.

3 (first turn)
Interviewer: Which direction has it moved so far?
Official: Later. Twice.

4 (first turn)
Interviewer: So "flexible" has only moved later.
Official: It's flexible in principle. It's moved later in practice.

5 (escalation body — new fact)
Interviewer: Is there a plan for it to move earlier at any point?
Official: There's a review process that could recommend that.

6 (escalation body — repetition with more strain)
Interviewer: Has the review process ever recommended moving it earlier?
Official: Not yet. It's a young process. Flexible, but young.

7 (escalation body — new fact)
Interviewer: How many reviews have there been?
Official: Four. All four recommended moving it later.

8 (close setup)
Interviewer: So every use of the flexibility has gone the same direction.
Official: Every use so far. That's what makes it worth calling flexible — there's room for that to change.

9 (close — flattest line)
Interviewer: Has anyone modelled what "earlier" would actually look like?
Official: Not as a priority, no.
```

Notice how "flexible" gets repeated and reframed across exchanges 2, 4, and 6 — each time carrying more strain — while exchanges 3, 5, and 7 each introduce a genuinely new fact (direction moved, a review process exists, four reviews have happened). The final line doesn't need a repeated word at all; it lands by being the calmest, most administratively bored answer in the piece.

## When a sketch has more than one absurd angle worth visiting

Some premises have two or three independent angles rather than a single escalating thread — for example, a "45-minutes-late still counts as on time" transport policy has an angle about what happens to actually-late trains, and a separate angle about how the on-time statistics will be reported. When a premise supports this, it's reasonable to devote a short block of exchanges to each angle in turn rather than force everything onto one continuous escalation line. Keep each angle's block internally paced (setup, one or two escalating facts, a mini-turn) and use the transition between angles as its own small escalation — the interviewer moving from "what happens to late trains" to "what do the statistics say" is itself a step up in scrutiny, not a reset.

## Signs a sketch has genuinely run out of material

Not every flat draft is a pacing problem — sometimes the premise itself doesn't have enough angles to sustain even four or five exchanges. A sign this is happening: after the setup and one escalation step, every subsequent question you can think of just restates "so it's absurd" in different words without adding a new fact, register, or consequence. When that happens, it's often more productive to go back to premise selection and either narrow the claim to something with a clearer internal contradiction, or widen it to touch a second consequence (cost, timeline, who's affected) that gives the escalation body something new to work with, rather than padding out exchanges that have nothing left to say.
