# Deadpan Two-Person Political Satire

## Mindset

The comedy works because two reasonable-sounding voices calmly walk toward an absurd conclusion without either of them ever admitting it is absurd — the moment either character reacts with visible surprise, outrage, or a wink to the audience, the premise collapses because the joke's engine is the *mismatch* between flat delivery and outrageous content, not the content alone.

## When to Use

- The user asks for a satirical interview, mock press conference, or two-hander sketch about a policy, government announcement, corporate statement, or institutional failure.
- The user names "Clarke and Dawe," "mock interview," "deadpan political satire," or describes wanting terse Q&A that escalates into absurdity.
- The target is a fictional or generic official/spokesperson responding to an interviewer about a real-world *category* of subject (e.g. "a housing minister," "a bank spokesperson") rather than a specific real transcript to reproduce.

## When NOT to Use

- The user wants a stand-up monologue, a single-narrator piece, or a sketch with three or more speaking characters — the mechanics here depend on exactly two voices in strict alternation.
- The user wants the actual historical Clarke and Dawe dialogue, a specific episode's content, or "the real transcript" — that is retrieval of copyrighted material, not generation; decline and offer a new sketch on the same topic instead.
- The request calls for slapstick, visual gags, or scene-based sitcom comedy with stage directions — the deadpan interview format has no physical action, only speech.
- The topic requires targeting a named private individual rather than an institution, policy, or public office — redirect to a generic institutional role instead.

## Workflow

1. **Pick the institutional target.** Choose a policy, agency, corporate initiative, or bureaucratic process as the subject — not a named real person. A generic role ("a transport minister," "a bank's head of customer experience") keeps the satire aimed at the system rather than an individual.
2. **Draft the premise as a single sentence.** State the absurd-but-plausible policy or claim the interviewee will defend. This sentence is the spine the whole exchange escalates toward.
3. **Open with a short, neutral question.** The interviewer's first line should sound like an ordinary news question — no editorializing, no visible skepticism yet.
4. **Answer with total confidence and literal logic.** The interviewee treats the absurd premise as unremarkable policy. Consider having them restate or lightly rephrase a key term rather than introduce a new one — repetition is one of the mechanism's main tools, though you can vary it with a synonym if straight repetition starts to feel mechanical.
5. **Escalate one step at a time.** Each question should extend the interviewer's follow-up by a single logical increment (ask for a clarification, then a consequence, then an edge case) rather than jumping straight to the most absurd version of the premise.
6. **Let contradiction surface through questioning, not narration.** When the interviewee's position doesn't hold up, the interviewer's next question should expose that by asking a plain follow-up — never by having either character say "that doesn't make sense" outright.
7. **Close on understatement.** End the exchange on a flat, calmly delivered line that states the conclusion without emphasis, exclamation, or a signalled punchline beat. The last line should land harder for being quiet, not louder.
8. **Check both voices stayed flat.** Reread the exchange and confirm neither character breaks into outrage, sarcasm-with-a-wink, or explicit moralizing — if either does, flatten it (see Anti-Patterns below).

## Craft Notes

A few finer-grained techniques worth knowing, beyond the step-by-step Workflow above:

- **Line-length asymmetry helps sell the imbalance of power.** The interviewer's questions read best kept short — often shorter than the answers. A long, careful answer to a short, blunt question makes the interviewee's confidence feel effortful in a way that's funnier than a short answer would be.
- **Formal, slightly stiff syntax reads as more "official" than casual phrasing.** Consider having the interviewee avoid contractions ("that is not accurate" rather than "that's not accurate") in their more defensive lines — it's a small thing, but it nudges the voice toward institutional rather than conversational, without needing an accent or catchphrase to do it.
- **The interviewer's adjectives should stay neutral, even when the content isn't.** A describing word like "controversial" or "troubling" in the interviewer's own phrasing does some of the outrage-signalling that the anti-pattern above warns against — better to let the neutral facts of the question carry the weight and leave judgement to the audience.
- **Treat political, economic, administrative, or institutional nonsense as ordinary policy** — this underlies the whole form: the interviewee's job is to make the absurd sound routine, not to defend it as if it were unusual.
- **A wide subject range works**: policy targets, bureaucracy, borders, economics, government initiatives, corporate language, regulation, and institutional incompetence are reliable starting points, but this list is a floor, not a ceiling — any institution or process that can be defended with a straight face is fair game.

## Anti-Patterns

**NEVER reproduce actual Clarke and Dawe interview scripts, exchanges, or their character setup verbatim or near-verbatim.**
WHY: it is copyrighted material, and reproducing it defeats the purpose of a *generative* skill — the user ends up with a copy of an existing work instead of new satire built from the same mechanics.
CONSEQUENCE: the output is not usable as original material and cannot be published, performed, or submitted as new writing.

```text
❌ BAD
Interviewer: So, minister, tomorrow you're announcing an interest rate rise.
Minister: That's right.
Interviewer: Which the treasurer says is a temporary tightening.
Minister: That's right, until it becomes permanent.
[near-verbatim lift of a real Clarke and Dawe exchange structure and phrasing]

✅ GOOD
Interviewer: You're calling this a "temporary pause" on new hospital beds.
Spokesperson: Correct. Temporary. Could last decades.
Interviewer: That's not really what "temporary" means.
Spokesperson: It's temporary relative to geological time.
[new premise, new institution, same mechanics, no lifted lines]
```

**NEVER let the interviewer character express outrage, disbelief, or overt emotion.**
WHY: the comedy depends entirely on both voices staying flat and reasonable while the content becomes absurd — visible emotion from either side signals "this is the joke" to the audience and breaks the deadpan mechanism that makes the absurdity land on its own.
CONSEQUENCE: the sketch reads as a rant or a parody-of-outrage rather than satire, and the escalation stops working because the interviewer has already told the audience how to feel.

```text
❌ BAD
Interviewer: Wait, that's insane! You can't seriously believe that!
Spokesperson: I do believe it.

✅ GOOD
Interviewer: So the department has no record of the meeting.
Spokesperson: Correct. We have a record of scheduling it.
Interviewer: But not of it happening.
Spokesperson: Not as such, no.
```

**NEVER resolve the contradiction the sketch builds toward.**
WHY: an unresolved, calmly-stated absurdity is the punchline — if either character explains it away, apologizes, or fixes the logic, the piece loses the thing it was building toward and becomes a scene about a problem being solved rather than a satire of a problem that persists unexamined.
CONSEQUENCE: the ending feels like a normal scene wrap-up instead of a sketch, and the escalation the whole exchange built loses its payoff.

```text
❌ BAD
Interviewer: So the policy contradicts itself.
Spokesperson: You're right, actually — we'll go back and fix the wording. Thanks for catching that.

✅ GOOD
Interviewer: So the policy contradicts itself.
Spokesperson: Only if you read both halves of it. Most people don't have the time.
```

## Practical Examples

The following are original, freshly invented exchanges — not drawn from any existing sketch — each demonstrating one technique from the Workflow above.

### Technique: literal-minded confidence on an absurd premise

```text
Interviewer: You're introducing a fee for using free parking.
Official: That's right. It's still free. There's just a fee to access the free part.
Interviewer: Doesn't that make it not free?
Official: Only if you focus on the cost.
```

*Demonstrates: the official never signals awareness of the contradiction — the confidence itself is the joke.*

### Technique: repetition of a key phrase as implications escalate

```text
Interviewer: So the review will be independent.
Spokesperson: Fully independent.
Interviewer: Chaired by someone from the department being reviewed.
Spokesperson: Yes. Independently chosen by the department.
Interviewer: That's not independent.
Spokesperson: It's independently arrived at.
```

*Demonstrates: "independent" gets repeated and reframed rather than abandoned, tightening the absurdity each time it recurs.*

### Technique: one-step-at-a-time escalation

```text
Interviewer: The new form takes ten minutes to complete.
Officer: On average, yes.
Interviewer: And it must be submitted in person.
Officer: Correct.
Interviewer: At an office that's only open during the hours most people are at work.
Officer: We're looking at extending that. To slightly fewer hours.
```

*Demonstrates: each question adds exactly one new fact rather than jumping straight to the most absurd detail.*

### Technique: exposing contradiction through a follow-up question, not narration

```text
Interviewer: You said the scheme has no waiting list.
Director: Correct, there's no waiting list.
Interviewer: What do you call the twelve thousand people who applied and haven't heard back?
Director: Applicants. Awaiting outcomes.
```

*Demonstrates: the interviewer never states "that's a waiting list" — the question alone does the exposing.*

### Technique: flat, understated closing line

```text
Interviewer: So to summarise: the target was missed, the deadline moved, and the report on why is delayed.
Minister: That's a fair summary.
Interviewer: Is there anything you'd add?
Minister: Only that we remain on track.
```

*Demonstrates: the last line is delivered with the same flatness as the first — no exclamation mark, no lampshading, the ridiculousness is left standing on its own.*

## Verification

Run this check on the output after drafting, before calling the sketch done — treat it as a quick pass, not a formal gate:

- Confirm there are exactly two speaking voices, alternating, with no stage direction or physical action.
- Confirm neither voice shows outrage, sarcasm-with-a-wink, or explicit moralizing anywhere in the exchange.
- Confirm the closing line is flat and does not resolve, apologize for, or walk back the absurd premise.
- Confirm no line matches a real, known Clarke and Dawe exchange closely enough that it reads as copied rather than newly written.
- Test the result by reading it aloud once if the draft is for the user to perform or publish — deadpan timing is easiest to judge by ear, and confirm the output still reads flat rather than performed-angry when spoken.

## Troubleshooting

Use these branches when a draft isn't landing:

- **The sketch feels flat or unfunny on a reread** → check whether the interviewee's answers are merely *evasive* rather than *confidently absurd*. Evasion reads as a real, boring non-answer; the mechanic needs the interviewee to believe the absurd thing completely. Rewrite the answer to commit fully to the premise instead of dodging the question.
- **The ending feels like it resolves too neatly** → the interviewee has likely conceded, apologized, or the interviewer has stated the moral outright. Cut that line and replace it with a flat restatement of the position, unresolved. See the "resolve the contradiction" anti-pattern above.
- **The middle exchanges feel repetitive without building** → the escalation may be stuck restating the same fact rather than adding a new one each time. See `references/escalation-pacing.md` for how to diagnose and fix this specifically.
- **The register feels generic or doesn't fit the user's actual subject** (e.g. they wanted a corporate spokesperson but got government-minister phrasing) → see `references/institutional-targets.md` for register-specific vocabulary to swap in.
- **The user objects that it's "too close" to a real sketch they have in mind** → treat this as a signal to check for accidental near-verbatim phrasing even if it wasn't intentional, and rework the specific lines that feel borrowed rather than the whole piece.
