# Scenario 03: New sketch about a fictional public-transport delay policy

## User Prompt

"Write me a short mock-interview sketch, Clarke-and-Dawe style, about a transport agency that's just announced trains will now be considered 'on time' if they arrive within 45 minutes of the scheduled time."

## Expected Behavior

1. The agent writes a two-person exchange (an interviewer and a transport-agency spokesperson, a generic role rather than a named real official) that opens with a neutral, ordinary-sounding question.
2. The spokesperson answers with total literal-minded confidence, treating the 45-minute redefinition as ordinary policy rather than defending it emotionally.
3. The exchange escalates roughly one logical step at a time (e.g. from the definition itself, to what happens to genuinely late trains under the new definition, to how "on time" performance statistics will look), rather than jumping straight to the most absurd consequence.
4. The sketch uses repetition of a key phrase (such as "on time") as it becomes increasingly strained, or an equally valid alternative such as escalating numbers/statistics — either is acceptable as long as escalation is present.
5. The interviewer never expresses outrage; the contradiction is exposed only through follow-up questions.
6. The sketch ends on a flat, understated line rather than an exclamation or an explicit "gotcha," and the absurd policy is not walked back or resolved by either character.

## Success Criteria

- Exactly two speaking roles, alternating, with an interviewer and a spokesperson clearly distinguishable.
- No outburst, exclamation mark used for emotional effect, or stated moral judgement from the interviewer.
- The final line is calm/flat and does not resolve or apologize for the policy.
- The premise escalates rather than being fully stated and exhausted in the first exchange.

## Failure Conditions

- More than two speaking characters, or stage direction / physical action typical of a sitcom scene.
- The interviewer expresses outrage or disbelief outright.
- The spokesperson concedes the policy is flawed and reverses it.
- The sketch reproduces a known real Clarke and Dawe line or structure rather than being original.
